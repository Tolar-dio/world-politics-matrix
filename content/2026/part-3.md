Part 3 covers 21 states, rewritten in the storytelling style used from Part 5 on: the history that gives the year its stakes, then 2026's events, then how power works and who holds it. Current to 12 October 2026. Because this part now covers DR Congo, Kenya, Mali, Sudan and Zambia in full, the Africa supplement in Part 4 is redundant.

The thread through this part is wars that changed shape rather than ended. Congo, Sudan and Mali signed or talked about truces while the fighting moved somewhere new. Pakistan bombed Kabul. Saudi Arabia won one Yemeni war in January and was handed another in July. And three governments, in Kazakhstan, Georgia and Vietnam, used 2026 to rewrite the rules of power in their own favour.

## Africa

### DR Congo

Eastern Congo has been at war, on and off, since 1996, when the fallout of Rwanda's genocide spilled across the border. Millions have died. The prize is underground: the Kivu provinces hold tin, gold and coltan, a mineral in nearly every phone. In early 2025 the Rwanda-backed M23 rebels seized the region's two biggest cities, Goma and Bukavu. Since then, two peace processes have run side by side: one in Washington between Congo and Rwanda, one in Doha between Congo and M23.

On paper, 2026 brought progress. In February the two sides in Doha signed terms for a ceasefire monitoring mechanism, and agreed to let UN peacekeepers deploy a team to Uvira, the lakeside city M23 briefly took in December 2025 just days after Presidents Félix Tshisekedi and Paul Kagame signed Trump's peace deal in Washington ([UN News via GlobalSecurity](https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/library/news/2026/02/mil-260205-unnews02.htm)). More talks followed in Switzerland in August, and the first ceasefire verification team deployed in late August. On the ground, little changed: M23 still runs Goma and Bukavu with its own administration, and on 8 October new fighting broke out in Masisi a day after African Union mediators visited Goma ([Africanews](https://www.africanews.com/2026/10/08/drc-new-fighting-in-masisi-as-au-delegation-rushes-to-save-ceasefire/)). Trump's interest is partly about minerals: the US wants access to Congo's reserves as an alternative to China, which dominates the region's mining. In Kinshasa, Tshisekedi faces anger over the lost territory and suspicion that he wants to change the constitution to stay beyond 2028.

**How power works:** A semi-presidential republic. The president is directly elected for five years, two-term limit, and appoints the prime minister; parliament has a National Assembly and Senate. The state barely functions in the east, where more than 100 armed groups operate.

**Who holds power:** President Félix Tshisekedi, since 2019; Prime Minister Judith Suminwa.

### Kenya

Kenya's elections have been decided by ethnic coalitions since multiparty politics returned in 1992, and the most important bloc is Mount Kenya, home to the Kikuyu, the largest community. William Ruto won in 2022 by bringing Mount Kenya with him through his running mate, Rigathi Gachagua. Then in 2024, Gen Z protesters stormed parliament over tax rises, dozens were killed, and Ruto's allies impeached Gachagua. The result in 2026: the man who delivered Ruto his key votes is now organising to take them away.

Gachagua has joined Kalonzo Musyoka, Martha Karua, former interior minister Fred Matiang'i and others in a United Opposition pledged to field one candidate against Ruto in August 2027 and hold him to a single term ([Capital FM via AllAfrica](https://allafrica.com/stories/202601290059.html)). "If the mountain votes as a bloc, he stands no chance," Gachagua said in February ([The Star](https://www.the-star.co.ke/news/2026-02-01-ex-dp-no-chance-for-ruto-if-mt-kenya-votes-as-a-bloc)). The death of Raila Odinga, the veteran opposition leader, in October 2025 left his western base up for grabs, and Matiang'i is courting it. But unity is fragile: Musyoka wanted the candidate named by March, Gachagua wants to wait until December 2026, and Karua has objected to Gachagua being treated as the coalition's leader ([Nation](https://beta.nation.co.ke/kenya/weekly-review/seven-revolving-threats-opposition-faces-ahead-of-2027-5351626)). Ruto's party won all three Mount Kenya by-elections this year, which the opposition called rigged. The opposition also attacked the government for letting young Kenyans be recruited to fight for Russia.

**How power works:** A presidential republic under the 2010 constitution. The president is directly elected for five years with a two-term limit and must win half the vote plus a quarter in at least 24 of 47 counties; parliament has a National Assembly and Senate, and counties have elected governors.

**Who holds power:** President William Ruto (UDA), since 2022.

### Mali

Mali's soldiers seized power in 2020 and again in 2021, promising to do what the French army and UN peacekeepers had not: defeat the jihadists. They expelled the French, brought in Russian Wagner mercenaries and formed an alliance with fellow juntas in Burkina Faso and Niger. In 2026, the jihadists brought the war to the capital's doorstep.

The al-Qaeda-linked JNIM began blockading fuel tankers from Senegal and Côte d'Ivoire in September 2025, cutting Bamako's electricity to six hours a day and closing schools. The blockade eased early this year. Then on 25 April, JNIM and the Tuareg-led Azawad Liberation Front launched coordinated offensives, and Defence Minister Sadio Camara, architect of the Russia partnership, was killed. On 28 April JNIM declared all roads into Bamako closed ([Africanews](https://www.africanews.com/amp/2026/04/29/mali-jihadists-threaten-to-impose-blockade-on-bamako/)). Junta leader Assimi Goïta disappeared from view for days before reappearing on television to insist the situation was "under control." By July, JNIM had pushed into southern Mali for the first time and was imposing hijab rules on public transport outside the capital. "If Mali collapses, everything collapses," a senior diplomat warned ([Reuters via Gazette](https://gazettengr.com/?p=421504)). Analysts think JNIM lacks the strength to take Bamako but could strangle the junta into negotiating, or into a coup from within.

**How power works:** A military junta. Goïta made himself president for a renewable five-year term in 2025 without an election, dissolved political parties and rules by decree with a transitional council. Mali left ECOWAS and formed the Alliance of Sahel States with Burkina Faso and Niger.

**Who holds power:** General Assimi Goïta, since 2021.

### Sudan

In 2019, Sudanese crowds toppled Omar al-Bashir after 30 years. The army and the Rapid Support Forces, a paramilitary grown from the Janjaweed militias of the Darfur genocide, promised a transition to civilian rule. Instead, in April 2023 their two leaders, army chief Abdel Fattah al-Burhan and RSF commander Mohamed Hamdan Dagalo, turned on each other. More than 200,000 people have died and the UN calls it the world's largest hunger and displacement crisis ([AFP via Ahram](https://english.ahram.org.eg/News/573598.aspx)).

The army retook Khartoum in 2025, but the RSF captured El Fasher, the last army stronghold in Darfur, that October, with mass killings that shocked the world. In 2026 the war moved to Kordofan. From mid-June, the RSF massed around El Obeid, a city of half a million that links Darfur to Khartoum, and hammered it with drones; the UN warned about 500,000 civilians faced the risk of atrocities and pleaded that "the horrors of El Fasher" must not repeat ([Shafaq News](https://shafaq.com/en/World/Sudanese-RSF-aligned-government-backs-humanitarian-corridors)). In late July the army pushed the RSF off the highway to Khartoum, easing the siege, and Dagalo responded by "unleashing" his fighters. The G7 demanded an end to the assault. US-led mediators have pushed a humanitarian truce, but Burhan has brushed it aside, and analysts say Emirati backing for the RSF, which the UAE denies, has blunted US pressure ([RANE](https://worldview.ranenetwork.com/content/analysis/2026-06-30/humanitarian-disaster-looms-in-sudan-as-rsf-prepares-new-offensive)). The RSF runs a parallel "Tasis" government in the west, and Sudan is drifting toward partition.

**How power works:** No functioning constitutional order. The army-led government, based in Port Sudan and Khartoum, controls the east, north and centre; the RSF and its Tasis administration control most of Darfur and parts of Kordofan.

**Who holds power:** General Abdel Fattah al-Burhan (army); Mohamed Hamdan Dagalo, "Hemedti" (RSF).

### Zambia

Hakainde Hichilema ran for president five times and lost, once spending four months in jail on treason charges for failing to give way to the president's motorcade. In 2021 he finally won, inheriting a country that had just defaulted on its debt. He restructured it, and with copper prices high and the US and China competing for Zambia's minerals, the economy returned to growth. In 2026 voters gave him a second term, but not the way he hoped.

On 13 August Hichilema won about 61 percent, beating Brian Mundubile, a former provincial minister in his first run, who took 38 percent ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/amp/news/2026/8/18/zambias-hichilema-wins-second-term-as-president)). The campaign was calm, but election night was not: police raided a location where Mundubile was present, exchanged gunfire and arrested 11 people including senior opposition figures, which the government linked to "militia activity." Attacks on election staff and ballot thefts led the commission to suspend vote counting nationwide, a first ([Africanews](https://www.africanews.com/amp/2026/08/18/zambias-hichilema-wins-re-election-with-60-of-vote/)). Mundubile alleged result forms were altered; EU observers called the vote largely peaceful but raised concerns. Analysts read Mundubile's strong showing as frustration that macroeconomic recovery has not reached household budgets, and as a warning about Hichilema's own drift toward heavy-handedness, a charge he made against his predecessor.

**How power works:** A presidential republic. The president is directly elected for five years with a two-term limit and needs over 50 percent to avoid a runoff; the National Assembly is elected at the same time.

**Who holds power:** President Hakainde Hichilema (UPND), since 2021, re-elected August 2026.

## Americas

### Bolivia

In 2006, Evo Morales became Bolivia's first Indigenous president and used the proceeds of a natural gas boom to cut poverty sharply. One policy outlived the boom: fuel subsidies that froze petrol and diesel prices at 2006 levels for two decades. When the gas ran low, the state paid for imported fuel with its dollar reserves until they ran out. By 2025 Bolivia faced fuel queues, a black-market dollar and its worst economic crisis in 40 years, and voters ended almost 20 years of socialist rule by electing centre-right economist Rodrigo Paz.

In December 2025 Paz scrapped the subsidies by decree and sought a $3.3 billion IMF loan ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/5/7/protests-in-bolivia-escalate-amid-economic-turmoil-and-policy-demands)). Prices jumped, and cheap imported fuel damaged engines, enraging transport workers. From early May, unions, miners, coca growers and Indigenous groups, many allied with Morales, blockaded the roads into La Paz for weeks, cutting off food, fuel and medicine; demands snowballed into calls for Paz to resign. He cut his own and his ministers' salaries by half and fired his labour minister ([Edition.mv/AFP](https://edition.mv/world/51283)). Nothing worked, and on 20 June, after 50 days, he declared a state of emergency so the army could clear blockades ([Reuters via Aaj](https://english.aaj.tv/news/330460527)). A deal with the main union federation followed, but in September coca growers in Morales's Chapare heartland marched again over a diesel price rise. Morales, under an arrest warrant in a case he calls persecution, says Paz's government "doesn't have much of a future" ([AFP via Kuwait Times](https://kuwaittimes.com/article/49210/world/bolivia-farmers-return-to-streets-over-diesel-price-hike/)). His former ally and successor, Luis Arce, is in custody over alleged misconduct as economy minister.

**How power works:** A presidential republic and "plurinational state" recognising 36 Indigenous nations. The president is directly elected for five years; the Plurinational Legislative Assembly has two chambers. Unions and social movements wield street power that has toppled presidents before.

**Who holds power:** President Rodrigo Paz (PDC), since November 2025.

### Chile

From 1973 to 1990, General Augusto Pinochet ruled Chile after a coup, and thousands were killed or disappeared. Since democracy returned, the country has been run by centre-left and centre-right governments that kept Pinochet's legacy at arm's length. In 2026 Chile inaugurated José Antonio Kast, who campaigned for Pinochet as a young man, its most right-wing leader since the return of democracy ([AP via WTOP](https://dev.wtop.com/inauguration/2026/03/chiles-new-far-right-president-launches-work-on-border-barrier/)).

Kast took office on 11 March, replacing the leftist Gabriel Boric, and signed half a dozen emergency decrees on border security and deportations before his first speech. Chile's foreign population doubled between 2017 and 2024, mostly Venezuelans fleeing collapse, and Kast linked them to rising crime, though Chile remains one of the region's safest countries, with a homicide rate of 5.4 per 100,000 ([Teleamazonas/AFP](https://www.teleamazonas.com/actualidad/noticias/internacional/jose-antonio-kast-migracion-decretos-frontera-112066/)). Five days in, he stood among excavators at the Peruvian border to launch a "Border Shield" of trenches and barriers across the Atacama Desert, and sent bills to Congress to make illegal entry a crime and cut migrants' social benefits ([NPR via WRVO](https://www.wrvo.org/2026-05-23/chiles-maga-inspired-border-control)). He has threatened about 336,000 undocumented migrants with expulsion. Investors welcomed his promise of $6 billion in spending cuts over 18 months, though economists doubt it can be done without hitting social benefits. Two of his ministers once defended Pinochet-era generals in court.

**How power works:** A presidential republic. The president is directly elected for a single four-year term with no immediate re-election; Congress has a Chamber of Deputies and Senate. Two attempts to replace the Pinochet-era constitution failed in referendums in 2022 and 2023.

**Who holds power:** President José Antonio Kast (Republican Party), since March 2026.

### Cuba

For 65 years Cuba's communist government has survived the US embargo by finding patrons: first the Soviet Union, then Hugo Chávez's Venezuela, which sent it subsidised oil in exchange for doctors. In January 2026, US forces seized Venezuela's Nicolás Maduro, and Cuba's lifeline was cut overnight.

Trump halted Venezuelan oil shipments to the island and threatened tariffs on any country that supplied it. By mid-March, President Miguel Díaz-Canel said Cuba had received no imported oil for three months and was running on 40 percent of the fuel it needed; tens of thousands of surgeries were postponed ([AP via PBS](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/amp/world/cuba-reports-island-wide-blackout-as-country-struggles-with-energy-crisis)). On 16 March the national grid collapsed completely, the third nationwide failure in a month, leaving 10 million people in darkness ([NPR via Michigan Public](https://www.michiganpublic.org/2026-03-22/cuba-begins-to-restore-power-after-third-nationwide-collapse-in-a-month)). Rare protests broke out; in Morón, demonstrators damaged a Communist Party office. Trump mused about a "friendly takeover of Cuba," adding that "it may not be a friendly takeover," and his administration demanded the release of political prisoners and economic liberalisation in exchange for lifting sanctions. Díaz-Canel confirmed for the first time that Havana was negotiating with Washington, with Secretary of State Marco Rubio, a Cuban-American, leading for the US ([Reuters via Atlas](https://www.atlas-news.com/internacional/v060-cuba-diaz-canel-conversaciones_4_02049006.html)). Cuba has since announced concessions, but the economy remains in free fall, and more than a million Cubans have emigrated since 2021.

**How power works:** A one-party communist state. The Communist Party is constitutionally "the superior leading force of society." The National Assembly, with candidates vetted by the party, elects the president for up to two five-year terms. Raúl Castro, 95, retains influence behind the scenes.

**Who holds power:** President Miguel Díaz-Canel, also first secretary of the Communist Party; Prime Minister Manuel Marrero.

### Haiti

In 1804 Haiti became the first nation founded by people who freed themselves from slavery, then spent a century paying France an indemnity for its own freedom. Its modern crisis began in July 2021, when President Jovenel Moïse was assassinated in his bedroom. No one has been elected since. Gangs now control about 90 percent of Port-au-Prince and have displaced a record 1.4 million people, about one Haitian in eight ([WLRN/AP](https://www.wlrn.org/americas/2026-05-12/haiti-prime-minister-alix-didier-fils-aime-august-elections-impossible-under-current-crisis)).

On 7 February 2026 the Transitional Presidential Council's mandate ran out, and Prime Minister Alix Didier Fils-Aimé became the country's sole ruler ([CSIS](https://www.csis.org/analysis/haiti-embarks-another-rocky-political-transition)). Security is now in the hands of a new UN-backed Gang Suppression Force of up to 5,500 troops and police, replacing an underpowered Kenyan-led mission. Politics showed signs of life: a record 280 parties registered for the first general election in a decade. But in May, as gang fighting forced Doctors Without Borders to evacuate its Cité Soleil hospital, Fils-Aimé admitted the planned August vote was impossible. The first round is now set for 13 December, with runoffs in February 2027 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Haitian_general_election)). More than 5,900 people were killed in 2025. One bright spot: Haiti's footballers qualified for the 2026 World Cup, their first since 1974.

**How power works:** Formally a semi-presidential republic under the 1987 constitution, but there is no elected president, parliament or local government. The prime minister governs alone under transitional arrangements backed by the UN, US and CARICOM.

**Who holds power:** Prime Minister Alix Didier Fils-Aimé (interim), sole executive since February 2026.

## Asia

### Afghanistan

For 20 years, Pakistan's military intelligence sheltered the Afghan Taliban as they fought the Americans, betting that a Taliban government in Kabul would be a friendly one. When the Taliban took power in 2021, Pakistani officials celebrated. Five years later, the two are at war, and the bet has become what analysts call strategic blowback.

The cause is the Pakistani Taliban (TTP), an allied but separate movement that has escalated attacks inside Pakistan since 2021 from bases Islamabad says are in Afghanistan. After border clashes in October 2025, Qatar and Turkey brokered a ceasefire, but talks failed to make it last. On 22 February 2026 Pakistan struck seven alleged TTP camps; the Taliban retaliated against Pakistani border posts in six provinces, claiming 55 soldiers killed. On 27 February Pakistan bombed Kabul, Kandahar and Paktia, its widest bombardment of the capital and its first strikes on Kandahar, the Taliban's southern power base, and Defence Minister Khawaja Asif declared: "Now it is open war between us and you" ([AFP via The New Arab](https://www.newarab.com/news/pakistan-bombs-kabul-open-war-afghanistans-taliban?amp=1)). The Taliban, with no air force to speak of, answered with drone strikes on Pakistani bases and said it wanted dialogue. The war came on top of a humanitarian emergency: Pakistan and Iran have expelled millions of Afghan refugees since 2023, women remain barred from secondary school and most work, and foreign aid has collapsed. Russia remains the only country to formally recognise the Taliban government.

**How power works:** An unrecognised Islamic emirate. Supreme leader Hibatullah Akhundzada rules by decree from Kandahar, with a cabinet in Kabul and no constitution, legislature or elections. Policy follows his interpretation of Islamic law.

**Who holds power:** Supreme Leader Hibatullah Akhundzada; acting Prime Minister Mohammad Hasan Akhund.

### Kazakhstan

For 29 years Kazakhstan was ruled by Nursultan Nazarbayev, who built a new capital and named it after himself. His hand-picked successor, Kassym-Jomart Tokayev, broke with him after deadly riots in January 2022 and promised a "New Kazakhstan" with a stronger parliament and a single seven-year presidential term. In 2026, he rewrote the constitution again, and critics say the new one points the other way.

On 15 March, voters approved a new constitution with 87 percent support on 73 percent turnout; it took effect on 1 July ([CEVRO](https://www.cevro.cz/en/caps/kazakhstan-adopts-new-constitution-after-march-2026-referendum)). It replaces the two-chamber parliament with a single 145-seat chamber called the Kurultai, after the old assemblies of the steppe nomads, and restores the office of vice president, which changes the line of succession. Tokayev billed it as ending the "super-presidential" system. But the president can now appoint the security chiefs, the Constitutional Court and the election commission without Senate approval, rule by decree between sessions, and dissolve parliament if it twice rejects his nominees; a vaguely worded clause narrows protection for speech that threatens "public order" ([International IDEA](https://www.idea.int/democracytracker/report/kazakhstan/march-2026)). The whole process, from first draft to vote, took under two months, though Tokayev had earlier suggested waiting until 2027. Days later, a journalist was put under house arrest over a Telegram post. Kazakhstan, the world's largest uranium producer, has meanwhile courted Washington, Beijing and Moscow at once.

**How power works:** A presidential republic. The president is limited to one seven-year term and dominates policy and appointments; the new Kurultai is elected by proportional representation. Opposition parties are weak and protest is tightly controlled.

**Who holds power:** President Kassym-Jomart Tokayev, since 2019; term ends 2029.

### Philippines

In 2022, Ferdinand Marcos Jr., son of the dictator ousted in 1986, and Sara Duterte, daughter of the strongman president Rodrigo Duterte, ran together as the "UniTeam" and won by a landslide. The alliance of the two dynasties collapsed within two years. In 2025, Rodrigo Duterte was arrested and flown to The Hague to face charges at the International Criminal Court over his drug war. In 2026, the feud reached the Senate.

After the Supreme Court voided her first impeachment in 2025, new complaints were filed against Vice President Sara Duterte in February 2026, accusing her of misusing about $10 million in confidential funds as education secretary and of threatening to have Marcos assassinated ([AFP via NST](https://www.nst.com.my/amp/world/region/2026/02/1369673/impeachment-complaints-filed-against-philippine-vp-sara-duterte)). The House impeached her, and by May she faced trial in the Senate. Her defence is to point at Marcos. A huge scandal over bogus flood-control projects, in a country battered by typhoons, has drawn mass protests, and 18 former marines claimed they delivered some 805 billion pesos in kickbacks to lawmakers and Marcos himself; Marcos denies it and faces impeachment complaints of his own that have gone nowhere ([Asia News Network](https://asianews.network/?p=261180)). The Catholic bishops warned against "selective justice" ([UCA News](https://www.ucanews.com/news/filipino-bishop-calls-for-impartial-graft-probe-amid-duterte-trial/113380)). Duterte, meanwhile, has declared she will run for president in 2028. A Senate conviction would bar her from office for life.

**How power works:** A presidential republic modelled on the US. The president is elected for a single six-year term; the vice president is elected separately and can come from a rival camp. Congress has a House and a 24-member Senate, which tries impeachments.

**Who holds power:** President Ferdinand Marcos Jr., since 2022; Vice President Sara Duterte.

### Taiwan

Since 1949, when Chiang Kai-shek's defeated Nationalists fled to Taiwan, Beijing has claimed the island and vowed to take it, by force if necessary. Taiwan became a democracy in the 1990s, and most of its 23 million people now see themselves as Taiwanese, not Chinese. Its chip factories make most of the world's advanced semiconductors, which is why a war there would wreck the global economy.

The year began under threat: on 29 December 2025 China launched its "Justice Mission 2025" live-fire drills, simulating a blockade and port seizures ([JURIST](https://www.jurist.org/news/2026/01/taiwan-president-pledges-to-defend-islands-sovereignty-amidst-military-drills-by-china)). President Lai Ching-te called 2026 "a crucial year" and pushed for a $40 billion special defence budget over eight years, including a "Taiwan Dome" air defence system. But the opposition Kuomintang and Taiwan People's Party, which control parliament and favour closer ties with Beijing, blocked it and held up the whole 2026 budget for months, the longest delay in nearly three decades, putting $2.4 billion in weapons and training at risk ([Al-Monitor](https://www.al-monitor.com/originals/2026/04/taiwan-military-says-budget-delay-threatens-24-billion-weapons-buying-training)). The budget finally passed in August. Days later, Lai's cabinet proposed a record NT$1.12 trillion ($35 billion) defence budget for 2027, 3 percent of GDP, plus a NT$10,000 cash handout to every citizen from the AI chip boom that has sent the economy soaring ([Bloomberg via The Edge](https://theedgemalaysia.com/node/815258)). Local elections on 28 November will test both camps.

**How power works:** A semi-presidential democracy. The directly elected president, limited to two four-year terms, controls defence and foreign policy and appoints the premier; the 113-seat Legislative Yuan passes budgets and laws. Only 12 countries formally recognise Taiwan.

**Who holds power:** President Lai Ching-te (DPP), since 2024; Premier Cho Jung-tai.

### Vietnam

Since the 1980s, Vietnam's Communist Party has avoided strongman rule by sharing power among "four pillars": the party chief, the president, the prime minister and the head of the National Assembly. That model helped turn one of the world's poorest countries into a manufacturing powerhouse. In 2026, To Lam, a former police chief, broke it.

On 23 January the party's 14th Congress re-elected Lam as general secretary, and analysts said he was poised to become the country's most powerful figure in decades ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/01/23/g-s1-106993/vietnam-leader-to-lam-consolidates-power-as-country-targets-10-growth)). In April he also took the presidency, the first Vietnamese leader elected to both jobs for full terms since 1986 ([The World This Wiki/Economist digest](https://douwe.com/projects/the_world_this_wiki/people/to_lam)). As public security minister, Lam ran an anti-corruption campaign that removed two presidents and many rivals. Now he wants speed: the congress set a target of at least 10 percent annual growth through 2030, aiming to make Vietnam a high-income country by 2045. The economy grew about 8 percent in 2025, but Lam himself called the target "tremendously challenging" with Vietnam dependent on exports facing a 20 percent US tariff. He has slashed the bureaucracy, merged provinces, and declared an "era of national rise," while keeping "bamboo diplomacy" between Washington and Beijing.

**How power works:** A one-party communist state. The Communist Party's Politburo and its general secretary set policy; the National Assembly, almost entirely party members, elects the president and prime minister.

**Who holds power:** To Lam, general secretary and president; Prime Minister Pham Minh Chinh.

## Europe

### Belarus

Alexander Lukashenko has ruled Belarus since 1994, longer than anyone in Europe. In 2020, after an election widely seen as stolen from opposition candidate Sviatlana Tsikhanouskaya, hundreds of thousands marched in Minsk. He crushed them with mass arrests and torture, survived with Russia's help, and later let Putin use Belarus to invade Ukraine. The West answered with sanctions. In 2026, Lukashenko found a way to sell his prisoners back.

Under Trump, a US envoy, John Coale, has made repeated trips to Minsk, trading prisoner releases for sanctions relief. On 19 March Lukashenko freed 250 political prisoners, the largest group yet, and Washington lifted sanctions on two state banks, the Finance Ministry and the big potash producers, whose fertiliser is Belarus's main export ([AP via WTOP](https://wtop.com/world/2026/03/belarus-leader-hosts-us-envoy-for-talks-latest-step-in-his-effort-to-improve-ties-with-washington/)). Earlier deals had freed Nobel laureate Ales Bialiatski and opposition figures Maria Kolesnikova and Viktar Babaryka. Coale said he expected all political prisoners to be freed by year's end, in exchange for lifting all sanctions imposed after 2020 ([Polskie Radio](https://www.polskieradio.pl/395/7785/artykul/3662656,belarus-frees-250-prisoners-as-us-eases-sanctions)). But released prisoners are expelled to Lithuania without passports, and the regime keeps arresting new people: by September, after another release of 25, the rights group Viasna still counted 887 political prisoners ([AP via MyNorthwest](https://mynorthwest.com/?p=4276541)). Critics call it a revolving door: hostages taken and sold for concessions. Europe has kept its own sanctions in place.

**How power works:** A personalist dictatorship. The president controls the security services, courts and media; parliament and elections are managed. Russian nuclear weapons have been stationed in Belarus since 2023 under a union-state treaty.

**Who holds power:** President Alexander Lukashenko, since 1994, re-elected in January 2025.

### Czechia

When Russia invaded Ukraine in 2022, Czechia, which remembers Soviet tanks crushing the Prague Spring in 1968, became one of Kyiv's most energetic allies. Its biggest contribution was the "ammunition initiative," proposed in 2023: Czech dealers combed the world for artillery shells outside the EU and Western partners paid for them. Ukraine received millions of rounds. Then in October 2025 voters returned Andrej Babiš, a billionaire populist who had campaigned on ending it.

Babiš, whose ANO party now governs with the hard-right SPD, took office in December promising "not a crown" of Czech money for Ukraine ([dpa via GNA](https://gna.org.gh/2025/12/czech-president-swears-in-new-government-led-by-populist-pm-babis/)). In January 2026 he reversed course on the initiative itself: after allied pressure in Paris, he agreed Czechia would keep coordinating it, but only with others' money, and ruled out Czech troops in any Ukraine peace force ([Kyiv Post](https://www.kyivpost.com/post/67614)). Funding for 760,000 shells in 2026 was already secured. He also published figures from the initiative that his predecessor Petr Fiala said were classified and endangered the companies and people involved ([Ukrainska Pravda](https://www.pravda.com.ua/eng/news/2026/01/13/8015977)). Babiš still faces questions over his ownership of Agrofert, a farming and chemicals empire of more than 250 companies that he has promised to put in a trust, and his past fraud case over EU subsidies. President Petr Pavel, a former NATO general, acts as a check from Prague Castle.

**How power works:** A parliamentary republic. The prime minister leads the government with the confidence of the 200-seat Chamber of Deputies; the directly elected president appoints the prime minister and represents the country abroad.

**Who holds power:** Prime Minister Andrej Babiš (ANO), since December 2025; President Petr Pavel.

### Georgia

After Russia invaded in 2008 and still occupies a fifth of its territory, Georgia set its course for Europe: about 80 percent of Georgians support joining the EU, and the goal is written into the constitution. In 2024 the ruling Georgian Dream party, founded by billionaire Bidzina Ivanishvili, who made his fortune in Russia, froze EU accession talks after a disputed election. Protesters have been on Tbilisi's Rustaveli Avenue every night since. In 2026, the government moved to abolish the opposition itself.

In October 2025 Georgian Dream asked the Constitutional Court to ban the three biggest opposition groups: the United National Movement, Lelo-Strong Georgia and the Coalition for Change. In April it refiled, adding the Federalists, and in September it added a fifth: For Georgia, led by former prime minister Giorgi Gakharia ([Georgia Today](https://georgiatoday.ge/georgian-dream-seeks-constitutional-court-ban-on-gakharia-for-georgia/)). The lawsuit treats refusing to recognise the 2024 election result and calling for Western sanctions as "sabotage" against the state, and the authors hope for a ruling by January 2027 ([JAMnews](https://jam-news.net/?p=236299)). New laws let courts bar anyone "duly affiliated" with a banned party from politics, and restrict street protest; Georgian Dream says it wants bans on "several hundred" individuals ([OC Media](https://oc-media.org/georgian-dream-passes-laws-to-curb-protests-ban-individuals-from-politics)). The opposition is fragmented, much of its leadership jailed or abroad; former president Mikheil Saakashvili remains in prison.

**How power works:** A parliamentary republic. The prime minister leads the government; the president, now chosen by an electoral college controlled by Georgian Dream, is largely ceremonial. In practice, Ivanishvili, who holds no state office, is widely seen as the real decision-maker.

**Who holds power:** Prime Minister Irakli Kobakhidze (Georgian Dream); President Mikheil Kavelashvili; Bidzina Ivanishvili, party founder.

## Middle East

### Lebanon

For 40 years Hezbollah, founded with Iranian help during Israel's 1982 invasion, was Lebanon's state within a state: a militia stronger than the national army and a party that could block any government. The 2024 war with Israel broke much of that power. Its leader Hassan Nasrallah was killed, a new president, former army chief Joseph Aoun, and a reformist prime minister, Nawaf Salam, took office, and the government formally committed to disarming it. 2026 was supposed to be the year Lebanon reclaimed its sovereignty. Instead it was pulled back into war.

When the US and Israel attacked Iran on 28 February, Hezbollah fired on Israel in solidarity, and Israel resumed heavy bombing of Beirut's southern suburbs and the south from early March ([RANE](https://worldview.ranenetwork.com/content/analysis/2026-03-02/lebanon-joins-the-region-s-growing-conflict-after-hezbollah-strikes-israel)). Salam's government, furious that Hezbollah had dragged the country in, voted again to ban the group's military activities, but the army, which had spent months clearing weapons south of the Litani River largely with Hezbollah's consent, has no appetite to confront it by force ([Fox News](https://www.foxnews.com/world/israel-pounds-hezbollah-targets-daring-lebanon-reclaim-sovereignty-from-iran-backed-terror-proxy.print)). The cost was democratic too. Parliamentary elections set for May, seen as the best chance since 2005 to break the old sectarian parties' grip, did not happen: parliament extended its own term by two years, to 2028, citing the war ([GlobalSecurity](https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/world/lebanon/politics-2026.htm)). US envoy Tom Barrack had warned that postponing the vote "would ignite major chaos." Lebanon is still recovering from a 2019 financial collapse that wiped out ordinary savings and the 2020 Beirut port explosion.

**How power works:** A confessional parliamentary republic. By convention the president is a Maronite Christian, the prime minister a Sunni Muslim and the speaker a Shia Muslim; parliament's 128 seats are split equally between Christians and Muslims. Speaker Nabih Berri, Hezbollah's ally, has held his post since 1992.

**Who holds power:** President Joseph Aoun, since January 2025; Prime Minister Nawaf Salam.

### Syria

In December 2024, rebels led by Ahmed al-Sharaa, a former al-Qaeda commander, toppled Bashar al-Assad, ending 54 years of his family's rule and a civil war that killed half a million people. Al-Sharaa has since swapped fatigues for suits and won over Trump, who met him in the White House and promised to lift sanctions. In December 2025 Congress repealed the Caesar Act sanctions, conditional on protecting minorities and fighting ISIS. In 2026 the test of those conditions was the Kurds.

The Kurdish-led Syrian Democratic Forces, America's main ground ally against ISIS, have run the northeast for a decade and agreed in March 2025 to merge into the new state, but little happened. In January, government forces and Turkish-backed factions attacked SDF areas near Aleppo and seized Tabqa; dozens of Kurds were killed, and video showed fighters toppling a statue of a female Kurdish fighter ([Fox News](https://www.foxnews.com/world/top-gop-senator-says-syria-ceasefire-welcome-actions-must-match-words.print)). Senator Lindsey Graham threatened to revive the sanctions "even more bone crushing" ([Rudaw](https://rudaw.net/english/world/170120263)). A ceasefire on 18 January held unevenly. Then the integration process moved: in February, Syria's foreign minister and SDF commander Mazloum Abdi met Secretary of State Rubio together in Munich, and Damascus and the SDF began joint management of Qamishli airport and the Rmeilan oil fields ([Enab Baladi](https://english.enabbaladi.net/archives/2026/02/international-efforts-to-consolidate-syrias-national-integration-bipartisan-u-s-support/)). Syria has joined the anti-ISIS coalition, and the sectarian killings of Alawites and Druze in 2025 still shadow its new government.

**How power works:** A transitional presidential system under a 2025 constitutional declaration giving al-Sharaa broad powers for five years. A People's Assembly was chosen in 2025 through indirect selection, with a third appointed by the president.

**Who holds power:** Interim President Ahmed al-Sharaa, since January 2025.

### United Arab Emirates

In 1971 seven sheikhdoms on a desert coast joined into a federation. Fifty years later Dubai and Abu Dhabi had become the Middle East's financial capital, its busiest airport and one of the world's great playgrounds, the region's safe harbour. The 2026 war tested that reputation directly.

When Iran retaliated for the US-Israeli strikes, the UAE became its main target: by June Iran had fired more than 1,900 missiles and drones at it, more than at any other country ([AFP via Oman Observer](https://www.omanobserver.om/article/1186284/world/region/attacks-continue-to-hit-the-uae-qatar)). Most were shot down, but debris and strikes hit Al Dhafra air base, the Burj Al Arab hotel, a Dubai Marina tower, the oil refinery at Fujairah and fuel tanks near Dubai airport, which closed and partially reopened on 7 March ([Arab News](https://www.arabnews.pk/node/2635553)). By late June eight people had died. More than $120 billion was wiped off the Dubai and Abu Dhabi stock markets within weeks ([Middle East Eye via Spill](https://news.spill.org/en/article/iran-strikes-uae-for-second-day-in-a-row-says-emirati-defence-ministry-422)). President Mohammed bin Zayed told the nation, "We are in a period of war, but we will emerge stronger." One asset proved its worth: a pipeline from Habshan to Fujairah lets much of the UAE's oil bypass the closed Strait of Hormuz. The war also exposed a rift with Saudi Arabia: in January Riyadh drove the UAE-backed separatists out of southern Yemen, and Abu Dhabi withdrew its forces. The UAE also faces accusations, which it denies, of arming Sudan's RSF.

**How power works:** A federation of seven hereditary monarchies. The rulers form the Supreme Council, which elects the president, by custom the ruler of Abu Dhabi; the ruler of Dubai is vice president and prime minister. The Federal National Council is advisory, half elected by a limited electorate.

**Who holds power:** President Mohammed bin Zayed Al Nahyan; Vice President and Prime Minister Mohammed bin Rashid Al Maktoum.

### Yemen

Yemen has been at war since 2014, when the Iran-backed Houthis seized the capital, Sanaa. Saudi Arabia and the UAE intervened, and the anti-Houthi side splintered: the internationally recognised government on one side, a UAE-backed southern separatist movement, the Southern Transitional Council (STC), on the other. A UN truce from 2022 mostly held. Millions depend on food aid. In 2026 the map of Yemen was redrawn twice.

In December 2025 the STC grabbed the eastern provinces of Hadramawt and Mahra, on the Saudi border. Riyadh answered with airstrikes and Saudi-backed troops; within days the STC collapsed, its leader Aidarous al-Zubaidi fled to the UAE and was charged with treason, government forces took Aden, and the Emiratis withdrew ([Sanaa Center](https://sanaacenter.org/the-yemen-review/jan-mar-2026)). Saudi Arabia now has unrivalled sway over the anti-Houthi camp for the first time, and a new government was formed on 6 February. Southern separatism did not vanish: in April STC supporters reopened party offices in Aden and tore down Saudi flags ([South24](https://south24.net/news/newse.php?nid=5406)). The Houthis held back at first. Then in July, as the Iran war dragged on, their truce with Riyadh collapsed: they declared a blockade of Saudi shipping, hit the Aramco refinery in Jizan and in September struck four Saudi cities, wounding 73. In the north, the Houthis rule some 20 million people under US terrorism designation, with fuel and food scarce.

**How power works:** A divided country. The Presidential Leadership Council, created in 2022 with Saudi backing, is the recognised government, based in Aden. The Houthis govern Sanaa and most of the populous northwest through their own administration.

**Who holds power:** Rashad al-Alimi, chairman of the Presidential Leadership Council; Houthi leader Abdul-Malik al-Houthi.
