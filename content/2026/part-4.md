Part 4 covers 19 states, rewritten in the storytelling style used from Part 5 on. Current to 13 October 2026. The original Part 4's Africa supplement (DR Congo, Kenya, Mali, Sudan, Zambia) is dropped because Part 3 now covers those countries in full; Madagascar, Somalia and Tanzania take its place.

The common thread is governments changing the rules to keep power. Bukele scrapped term limits and moved the next election up. Somalia's president extended his own mandate. Kuwait suspended parliament and stripped 71,000 people of citizenship. Tanzania put the opposition leader on trial for his life. The counter-examples matter as much: Guatemala finally replaced an attorney general who tried to stop an elected president, and Romania's parliament ousted a prime minister for doing unpopular things that needed doing.

## Africa

### Madagascar

Madagascar is home to animals found nowhere else on Earth, and to one of its poorest populations: about three in four people live below the poverty line, and power and water cuts are routine in the capital. Its politics has swung between elections and coups since independence from France in 1960. In 2009 a young DJ-turned-mayor, Andry Rajoelina, took power with the backing of an elite army unit called CAPSAT. In October 2025 the same unit took power from him.

Youth-led "Gen Z" protests over water and electricity shortages began on 25 September 2025. On 11 October, CAPSAT commander Colonel Michael Randrianirina, who had been jailed in 2023 for allegedly plotting a coup, called on soldiers to refuse orders to fire on protesters. Rajoelina fled, parliament impeached him, and Randrianirina took power on 14 October, promising elections within 18 to 24 months ([AFP via eNCA](https://www.enca.com/news/madagascar-enters-military-rule-after-colonel-seizes-power-0)). The African Union suspended Madagascar. In 2026, the questions have been whether the colonel means it. He submitted a transition roadmap to the regional bloc SADC by its 28 February deadline, with presidential elections planned for 2027. Then on 9 March he abruptly sacked his prime minister, the businessman he had appointed in October, and the entire cabinet, without explanation ([People Daily](https://peopledaily.digital/news/madagascars-military-leader-dissolves-govt-in-a-surprise-move/amp)). Gen Z leaders who cheered the takeover say they want more say, and analysts describe protesters' "buyer's remorse" at a revolution they fear has been hijacked ([ISS Africa](https://issafrica.org/iss-today/the-madagascar-upheaval-coup-revolution-or-coupvolution)). The record of Africa's other recent juntas, almost all of which missed their own election deadlines, does not encourage optimism.

**How power works:** A military-led transition. Randrianirina, confirmed as head of state by the High Constitutional Court, governs with a committee of army, gendarmerie and police officers and an appointed transitional government; parliament's role is limited.

**Who holds power:** Colonel Michael Randrianirina, since October 2025.

### Somalia

Somalia has had no strong central government since the dictator Siad Barre fell in 1991. Since 2004, its leaders have been chosen not by voters but by clan elders who pick MPs who pick the president. Al-Shabaab, al-Qaeda's affiliate, still controls much of the countryside, and a 2025 offensive reversed government gains and put it within reach of Mogadishu ([Africa Center for Strategic Studies](https://africacenter.org/spotlight/en-elections-2026/somalia/)). In 2026, the fight over how, and whether, to hold elections pushed the country toward another constitutional crisis.

President Hassan Sheikh Mohamud wants Somalia's first one-person, one-vote national elections since 1969. His opponents, including the leaders of the powerful federal states of Puntland and Jubaland, suspect the reform is cover for staying in power. On 4 March, parliament voted by acclamation to extend the terms of MPs and the president from four years to five, pushing elections back a year ([Reuters via Polity](https://www.polity.org.za/article/somali-parliament-approves-constitution-change-to-extend-presidents-term-delay-election-2026-03-05)). The opposition Somali Future Council, which includes two former presidents, declared that parliament's mandate expired on 14 April and the president's on 15 May, and threatened to hold its own parallel election ([Dawan Africa](https://www.dawan.africa/news/somali-future-council-threatens-separate-election-timeline)). A similar term extension in 2021 split the security forces along clan lines and led to fighting in Mogadishu. Analysts warn of a possible federal military move on Jubaland, while the UAE, Turkey, Egypt and Ethiopia all compete for influence along Somalia's coast. About 11,000 African Union troops remain in the country.

**How power works:** A federal parliamentary republic under a provisional constitution. Parliament elects the president, who appoints a prime minister. The federal member states, especially Puntland and Jubaland, act with wide autonomy; Somaliland in the north has operated as a separate, unrecognised state since 1991.

**Who holds power:** President Hassan Sheikh Mohamud, re-elected 2022, mandate disputed since May 2026; Prime Minister Hamza Abdi Barre.

### Tanzania

For six decades Tanzania was East Africa's model of stability: the country of Julius Nyerere, never a coup, never a civil war, ruled throughout by the same party, CCM. When Samia Suluhu Hassan became its first woman president in 2021, after the death of the authoritarian John Magufuli, she promised reconciliation and loosened his crackdown. By the October 2025 election, she had reversed course.

The main opposition party, Chadema, was barred from the vote, and its leader Tundu Lissu, who survived 16 bullets in a 2017 assassination attempt, was arrested in April 2025 and charged with treason for campaigning under the slogan "No reforms, no elections." Samia won 98 percent. The largest protests in Tanzania's history followed, met with an internet blackout and mass killings; the opposition says about 700 or more died, rights groups say thousands ([Gazette](https://gazettengr.com/tanzanian-govt-charges-over-70-for-treason-after-deadly-election-violence/)). In 2026 the reckoning has been one-sided. A government commission acknowledged 518 deaths but blamed "trained agitators" and "outside forces" and praised police restraint, findings even ruling-party members privately call ridiculous ([The World This Wiki/Economist digest](https://mobile.douweosinga.com/projects/the_world_this_wiki/people/samia_suluhu_hassan)). Hundreds were charged with treason. Lawyers and rights groups have asked the International Criminal Court to investigate. On 21 August a court ruled Lissu has a case to answer; he faces a possible death sentence, represents himself, and has asked to call Samia as a defence witness ([AFP via Africanews](https://www.africanews.com/amp/2026/08/21/tanzanian-opposition-leader-to-face-trial-on-treason-charges/)). The government's priority is growth and a long-delayed $40 billion gas project.

**How power works:** A presidential republic dominated by CCM since independence. The president is directly elected for up to two five-year terms and appoints a prime minister; Zanzibar has its own semi-autonomous government.

**Who holds power:** President Samia Suluhu Hassan (CCM), since 2021; Prime Minister Mwigulu Nchemba.

## Americas

### Ecuador

Ten years ago Ecuador was one of South America's safer countries, wedged between the world's two biggest cocaine producers, Colombia and Peru, but largely spared their violence. Then its ports became the main exit for cocaine to Europe and the US, and the gangs followed. By 2025 it had the highest murder rate in Latin America, about 52 per 100,000, roughly one killing an hour ([AFP via FMT](https://fmtv5.freemalaysiatoday.com/category/world/2026/01/22/ecuador-announces-trump-style-tariffs-on-colombia-over-drugs)). Daniel Noboa, a banana-fortune heir elected at 35, has answered with soldiers, prisons and a close alliance with Trump. In November 2025 voters rejected his plan to allow foreign military bases, a sharp personal rebuke.

In 2026 Noboa took the war across his border and into trade. In January he blamed Colombia's leftist president Gustavo Petro for failing to fight cocaine and illegal gold mining along the frontier, and slapped a 30 percent "security tariff" on Colombian goods; he raised it to 50 percent in March and 100 percent from 1 May ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/4/9/ecuador-hikes-tariffs-to-100-percent-in-feud-with-neighbour-colombia)). Petro called it a "monstrosity" that meant the end of the Andean trade pact for Colombia, and cut off cross-border electricity sales that Ecuador depends on during droughts. On 3 March Noboa and Trump launched a joint military operation in Ecuador against what the US calls designated terrorist organisations; Petro alleged bombs fell near farms on the border ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/4/1/ecuador-claims-28-drop-in-homicides-amid-concerns-over-anti-crime-campaign)). The government says killings are finally falling: 14 percent fewer in the first quarter, 28 percent fewer in March, though 1,800 people had still been murdered by early April ([El Diario](https://www.eldiario.ec/centro/presidente-noboa-reporta-1-800-homicidios-en-ecuador-en-2026-y-cuestiona-a-la-onu-09042026/)). Critics say the war targets the poor while money launderers go untouched.

**How power works:** A presidential republic. The president is directly elected for four years with one re-election and can dissolve the unicameral National Assembly once, triggering new elections for both. Ecuador uses the US dollar as its currency.

**Who holds power:** President Daniel Noboa (ADN), since November 2023, re-elected April 2025.

### El Salvador

In 2015 El Salvador was the murder capital of the world, with gangs like MS-13 controlling whole neighbourhoods. Nayib Bukele, a former advertising executive who calls himself the world's "coolest dictator," declared a state of exception in 2022 and has kept it renewed ever since, jailing more than 80,000 people, about 1 in 60 adults, many without trial. Murders collapsed, and Bukele became the most popular leader in the hemisphere. He has used that popularity to remove every limit on his power.

In 2024 he won a second term despite a constitutional ban, after judges his party appointed reinterpreted the rule. In August 2025, his party's legislators, holding 57 of 60 seats, approved indefinite presidential re-election, extended the term to six years and scrapped runoffs ([AP via Fox Baltimore](https://foxbaltimore.com/news/nation-world/el-salvador-allows-indefinite-reelection-extends-presidential-terms-to-6-years-nayib-bukele-legislative-assembly-street-gangs-constitutional-rights)). They also cut his current term short by two years, moving the next general election to March 2027, so he can win a longer term sooner. "Democracy in El Salvador has died," said one of the three opposition deputies who voted no. Human rights lawyers and journalists who documented abuses in his prisons, including the anti-corruption lawyer Ruth López, have been arrested or forced into exile ([News Central](https://newscentraltv.com/?p=353346)). His mega-prison CECOT, which houses deportees sent by the Trump administration, has made him Washington's most useful ally in the region. In 2026 the country is effectively in campaign season for a vote whose result few doubt.

**How power works:** Formally a presidential republic; in practice power is concentrated in Bukele, whose New Ideas party controls the Legislative Assembly, the Supreme Court and the attorney general's office. Constitutional guarantees remain suspended under the state of exception.

**Who holds power:** President Nayib Bukele (Nuevas Ideas), since 2019.

### Guatemala

Guatemala's 36-year civil war killed some 200,000 people, most of them Indigenous Maya, before peace accords in 1996, whose 30th anniversary falls this year. A UN-backed anti-corruption commission, CICIG, later jailed a sitting president, until the elite it targeted had it expelled in 2019. In 2023 voters elected Bernardo Arévalo, an anti-corruption sociologist and son of the country's first democratic president. The attorney general, Consuelo Porras, then spent months trying to stop him taking office and the next two years prosecuting his party and allies.

Guatemala's president cannot fire the attorney general, so Arévalo waited. In 2026 the wait ended. Porras, sanctioned by 40 countries for obstructing anti-corruption work, was left off the shortlist for another term, and on 5 May Arévalo named lawyer Gabriel García Luna to replace her when her term ended on 17 May ([AP via WTOP](https://wtop.com/world/2026/05/guatemalas-president-replaces-attorney-general-after-years-long-struggle/)). "The Public Ministry is getting a new authority who does not come to serve a president," Arévalo said. Human Rights Watch noted that under Porras, convictions against cartels and organised crime fell sharply ([HRW](https://www.hrw.org/news/2026/04/29/arevalos-narrow-path-to-change-in-guatemala)). For survivors of the war's massacres, whose cases stalled under her, her exit was "a sigh of relief" ([JusticeInfo](https://www.justiceinfo.net/en/158869-guatemala-the-fall-of-consuelo-porras.html)). The catch: Arévalo has barely a year left before the 2027 election and a Congress still dominated by the old parties, and he cannot run again.

**How power works:** A presidential republic. The president is directly elected for a single four-year term; the 160-seat Congress is fragmented. The attorney general is appointed for four years from a commission shortlist and cannot be removed by the president.

**Who holds power:** President Bernardo Arévalo (Semilla), since January 2024.

### Honduras

Honduras has one of the most turbulent democracies in the Americas: a coup in 2009 against President Manuel Zelaya, a disputed election in 2017, and a former president, Juan Orlando Hernández, convicted in New York in 2024 of helping traffic more than 400 tonnes of cocaine. In 2022 Zelaya's wife Xiomara Castro became the first woman president on a leftist platform. The 2025 election to replace her became one of the most contested in the region's recent history, and Washington put its thumb on the scale.

Days before the 30 November vote, Trump endorsed Nasry "Tito" Asfura, a construction magnate and former Tegucigalpa mayor from Hernández's National Party, and attacked the rival candidates; he also pardoned Hernández. Counting took nearly a month. Asfura was declared the winner with 40.27 percent to 39.53 percent for TV personality Salvador Nasralla of the Liberal Party, a margin of fewer than 30,000 votes out of 3.7 million ([AP via News4Jax](https://www.news4jax.com/news/world/2026/01/27/trump-backed-businessman-nasry-asfura-sworn-in-as-president-of-honduras/)). Castro's party, Libre, came a distant third with about 19 percent ([El Nuevo Diario](https://elnuevodiario.com.do/asfura-asumira-el-poder-en-honduras-en-ceremonia-sencilla-y-sin-ostentacion-en-2026/)). Both Nasralla and Libre alleged fraud, and Nasralla still says he is the rightful president. Asfura took office on 27 January in a deliberately austere ceremony in Congress, without foreign heads of state, promising to shrink the state and "confront insecurity head-on." As one analyst noted, all three major parties have been credibly linked to drug money ([World Politics Review](https://www.worldpoliticsreview.com/asfura-honduras-president-inauguration/)).

**How power works:** A presidential republic. The president is directly elected for a single four-year term by simple plurality; the 128-seat National Congress is elected at the same time.

**Who holds power:** President Nasry Asfura (National Party), since 27 January 2026.

## Asia

### Cambodia

The border between Cambodia and Thailand was drawn by French colonial surveyors in 1904-07, and the two countries have fought over the line, and the ancient Khmer temples along it, ever since. In 2025 the dispute exploded into the worst fighting in decades: clashes in July and again in December killed dozens and displaced more than a million people on both sides, and Thai jets bombed casinos and scam compounds inside Cambodia ([The Vibes/AFP](https://www.thevibes.com/articles/world/120002/hun-manet-pushes-for-border-peace-with-thailand-and-vows-crackdown-on-cyberscam-centres)). Trump brokered a peace accord, which he still cites as one of his successes.

In 2026 the peace is cold. In February Prime Minister Hun Manet, a West Point graduate who inherited the job from his father Hun Sen in 2023, gave his first interview to international media and said Thai forces were occupying Cambodian land "further beyond even Thailand's own unilateral claim," urging Bangkok to let a joint boundary commission resume work ([Reuters via NST](https://www.nst.com.my/amp/world/region/2026/02/1380104/cambodian-pm-says-thailand-occupying-territory-after-trump-brokered)). In Thailand, Anutin Charnvirakul rode nationalist anger over the war to a landslide in February. Cambodia's other problem is the one Thailand used to justify its strikes: industrial-scale cyber scam compounds, where trafficked workers are forced to defraud people worldwide. Parliament passed a law against the scam rings, and Hun Manet admitted of one accused tycoon, "We did not know that he was the kingpin." Critics note that many compounds are linked to well-connected businessmen. Meanwhile Hun Sen, as Senate president, remains the power behind his son; analysts noted he, not Hun Manet, ran the 2025 war from his "war room."

**How power works:** A constitutional monarchy under King Norodom Sihamoni, in practice a one-party state. The Cambodian People's Party holds almost every seat after the main opposition was dissolved in 2017. The Hun family dominates government, the military and business.

**Who holds power:** Prime Minister Hun Manet, since 2023; Senate President Hun Sen.

### Malaysia

In 2015 investigators found that some $4.5 billion had been looted from 1MDB, a state investment fund, with $700 million landing in the personal bank account of Prime Minister Najib Razak. It became one of the biggest financial scandals in history, helped topple the party that had ruled Malaysia since independence, and in 2022 put Najib in prison. That same year, Anwar Ibrahim, the reformer who had himself been jailed twice on charges widely seen as political, finally became prime minister after 25 years of trying, but only by allying with Najib's party, UMNO. In 2026 that bargain is coming due.

With a general election due by early 2027, Anwar's unity government is beset by corruption controversies, ethnic tensions and unease among both Malay and Chinese voters ([East Asia Forum](https://eastasiaforum.org/2026/02/08/malaysia-enters-election-mode-in-2026/)). Najib, 73, his sentence already halved by a royal pardon board in 2024, has kept fighting to serve the rest under house arrest, citing a disputed royal addendum. In September, as UMNO held its annual assembly, the pardons board met again to consider it; Anwar was reportedly upset not to have been told in advance, and the law minister and attorney general were said to oppose more clemency ([The Vibes](https://www.thevibes.com/articles/news/127118/anwar-surprised-by-najibs-new-pardon-attempt)). For Anwar, freeing Najib would shred his anti-corruption credentials; refusing would anger the coalition partner he needs. Sabah and Sarawak, the resource-rich states on Borneo, are pressing for more autonomy and oil revenue, and Malaysia's electronics exports still face a 19 percent US tariff.

**How power works:** A federal constitutional monarchy. The king is chosen every five years in rotation from nine hereditary state rulers and holds limited but real powers, including pardons. The prime minister leads the coalition with a majority in the 222-seat lower house.

**Who holds power:** Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim (PKR), since 2022; King Sultan Ibrahim of Johor.

### North Korea

The Kim family has ruled North Korea since 1948, the world's only communist hereditary dynasty, sustained by a personality cult around the "Paektu bloodline" named after the sacred mountain where Kim Jong Il was supposedly born. Kim Jong Un, now 42, inherited power at 27. In 2026 he took the clearest step yet toward naming a fourth-generation heir, and she is a girl of about 13.

In February, South Korea's intelligence agency told lawmakers that Kim's daughter Ju Ae had been clearly "designated as a successor" ([AFP via Citizen Digital](https://www.citizen.digital/news/north-koreas-kim-jong-un-picks-teenage-daughter-as-heir-n377371)). In January state media showed her for the first time at the Kumsusan mausoleum where her great-grandfather and grandfather lie embalmed. The Workers' Party held its Ninth Congress from 19 February, its first in five years, replacing more than half its presidium; Ju Ae was not shown in the hall ([Financial News](https://en.fnnews.com/news/202602201055191591)). But at the closing military parade on 25 February she appeared at her father's side in a matching leather coat, walking the red carpet as generals saluted ([AFP via Watauga Democrat](https://www.wataugademocrat.com/news/national/dressed-for-succession-kim-jong-un-daughter-fuel-speculation-with-matching-coats/article_1da17d1b-6502-5f54-9315-4bf98530d53d.html)). Experts caution she is too young even for party membership, and Kim's sister Kim Yo Jong remains powerful. Meanwhile North Korea has never been more secure: it has thousands of troops fighting for Russia, receives Russian technology and money in return, and its nuclear arsenal keeps growing.

**How power works:** A totalitarian one-party state. Kim Jong Un is general secretary of the Workers' Party, chairman of the State Affairs Commission and commander of the armed forces; no independent institutions exist. Information is tightly controlled, and political prisoners number in the tens of thousands.

**Who holds power:** Kim Jong Un, since 2011.

### Sri Lanka

In 2022 Sri Lanka ran out of dollars to buy fuel, food and medicine, defaulted on its debts, and crowds stormed the presidential palace and swam in the president's pool. In 2024 voters swept away the dynasties that had run the country for decades and elected Anura Kumara Dissanayake, leader of a Marxist party that once led armed uprisings, who then won a two-thirds majority in parliament. By late 2025 his government had become an IMF success story. Then the weather struck.

Cyclone Ditwah hit on 26 November 2025 and its floods and landslides killed more than 640 people, affected 2.3 million, over a tenth of the population, and touched all 25 districts; the total damage is thought to exceed the 2004 tsunami's ([AFP via eNCA](https://www.enca.com/business/sri-lanka-plans-16-bn-cyclone-recovery-spending-2026)). Dissanayake called it the most challenging natural disaster in the island's history. Officials estimated reconstruction at $6 to 7 billion. In 2026 the government is trying to rebuild without breaking the fragile recovery: it approved 500 billion rupees, about $1.6 billion, in extra spending without raising its borrowing limit, offered survivors up to 10 million rupees to resettle on safer land, and secured emergency IMF funding of $206 million. In January the IMF confirmed its bailout would continue unchanged, citing the government's "strong fiscal discipline" ([Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/world-news/imf-extended-fund-facility-in-sri-lanka-s-post-ditwah-recovery-to-continue-126012801257_1.html)). Analysts warn the shock shows how exposed a debt-distressed country remains ([East Asia Forum](https://eastasiaforum.org/?p=2348671)).

**How power works:** A semi-presidential republic. The directly elected president, limited to two five-year terms, heads state and government and appoints the prime minister and cabinet; the 225-seat parliament is elected by proportional representation.

**Who holds power:** President Anura Kumara Dissanayake (National People's Power), since 2024; Prime Minister Harini Amarasuriya.

## Europe

### Azerbaijan

For 30 years Azerbaijan and Armenia fought over Nagorno-Karabakh, a mountainous enclave inside Azerbaijan populated mostly by Armenians. Armenia won the first war in the 1990s; Azerbaijan, rich with Caspian oil and gas, won it back in 2020 and in 2023 seized the rest in a one-day offensive that drove out almost the entire Armenian population of about 100,000. Having won, President Ilham Aliyev, who inherited power from his father in 2003, has spent 2026 selling peace.

In August 2025, at the White House, the two countries initialled a peace agreement and agreed on the "Trump Route for International Peace and Prosperity" (TRIPP): a US-run transit corridor through southern Armenia linking Azerbaijan to its exclave of Nakhchivan and on to Turkey, the route Baku had long demanded under the name Zangezur Corridor ([CEPA](https://cepa.org/article/trumps-road-tripp-delivers-a-peace-deal/)). In 2026 the deal started to change facts on the ground. Aliyev declared that "peace has already been achieved" and that the two sides are "just learning to live in peace"; he lifted a ban on transit to Armenia, and from December 2025 Azerbaijani fuel began reaching Armenia by rail through Georgia ([Carnegie Endowment](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/03/rewiring-the-south-caucasus-tripp-and-the-new-geopolitics-of-connectivity)). In August he wrote to Trump that relations had reached "their highest point in the 34-year history" ([Eurasianet](https://eurasianet.org/assessing-armenian-azerbaijani-peace-deal-one-year-later)). The catch is a precondition: Baku will not sign the final treaty until Armenia rewrites its constitution to drop any claim to Karabakh, which Prime Minister Pashinyan plans to put to a referendum. TRIPP also cuts Russia out of a region it long policed, and Aliyev's relations with Moscow have soured badly.

**How power works:** A presidential republic in name, a hereditary autocracy in practice. The president has no term limits and appoints the vice president, his wife Mehriban Aliyeva, who would succeed him. Parliament is loyal, independent media have been crushed, and critics are routinely jailed.

**Who holds power:** President Ilham Aliyev, since 2003.

### Iceland

Iceland, with 390,000 people, is the smallest country in NATO and the only one with no army. In 2008 its three big banks collapsed in one week, and in the wreckage it applied to join the EU, then dropped the bid in 2013 when a eurosceptic government came in. The reason it walked away then, and the reason it walked away again in 2026, is fish: fisheries are the backbone of the economy and Icelanders fear Brussels controlling their waters.

Prime Minister Kristrún Frostadóttir, a 38-year-old economist whose pro-EU coalition took power in late 2024, argued that the geopolitical moment had changed. Trump's threats to annex Greenland, Iceland's neighbour, made many Icelanders wonder whether NATO and a bilateral US defence pact were still enough ([Journal-News/AP](https://www.journal-news.com/nation-world/iceland-eu-election/image_539481b8-32f7-5f3e-b332-72fc379ea61f.html)). She called a referendum, not on joining but on reopening talks, with a second vote promised on any final deal. On 29 August, Icelanders said no: 52.8 percent against, 47.2 percent for ([NPR via WYPR](https://www.wypr.org/2026-08-29/icelanders-vote-no-to-talks-aimed-at-joining-the-eu-in-close-referendum)). Worries about fishing quotas and giving up the króna for the euro outweighed the security case. Frostadóttir accepted the result and said the issue will not return before her term ends in 2028. Iceland remains in the European Economic Area and Schengen, already following, by her count, about 75 percent of EU rules without a vote on them. EU officials had hoped a yes would boost morale for the bloc's enlargement.

**How power works:** A parliamentary republic. The prime minister leads the government with the confidence of the 63-seat Althing, one of the world's oldest parliaments, founded in 930. The directly elected president is largely ceremonial.

**Who holds power:** Prime Minister Kristrún Frostadóttir (Social Democratic Alliance), since December 2024; President Halla Tómasdóttir.

### Moldova

Moldova, wedged between Romania and Ukraine, is one of Europe's poorest countries and one of its most contested. Russian troops have sat in its breakaway eastern strip, Transnistria, since a 1992 war. President Maia Sandu has set it on course for the EU, and her party held its majority in the September 2025 election against what officials described as a massive Russian vote-buying and disinformation operation. In 2026, the question has been whether Brussels can deliver its side.

Moldova's path is chained to Ukraine's: Hungary's veto on opening negotiating chapters for Ukraine blocked Moldova too. In March 2026 the EU found a workaround, informally opening technical work on the negotiating clusters without the unanimous political vote, and Sandu has named 2028 as a target for accession ([Gariwo](https://en.gariwo.net/magazine/europe-and-democracy/chiinu-accelerates-towards-eu-despite-transnistria-impasse-30221.html)). Viktor Orbán's defeat in Hungary in April may remove the main obstacle. Meanwhile Russia's energy weapon has backfired on its own client: since Gazprom cut gas in January 2025, Transnistria has rationed gas and suffered blackouts, its economy shrank about 18 percent and industrial output more than 30 percent in 2025, the worst crisis since its founding war ([OSW](https://www.osw.waw.pl/en/publikacje/analyses/2026-02-17/moldova-a-convergence-fund-instead-a-reintegration-plan)). Chișinău, now powered via Romania, has offered a "convergence fund" for the region, but analysts warn it is unprepared if Transnistria suddenly collapses and has to be absorbed.

**How power works:** A parliamentary republic. The prime minister leads the government backed by the 101-seat parliament; the directly elected president shapes foreign policy. Transnistria and the autonomous region of Gagauzia are both strongholds of pro-Russian sentiment.

**Who holds power:** President Maia Sandu (PAS), since 2020; Prime Minister Alexandru Munteanu.

### Romania

In December 2024 Romania's Constitutional Court annulled a presidential election after a little-known far-right candidate, Călin Georgescu, surged to first place on a TikTok campaign that intelligence services linked to Russia. In the rerun, the pro-European mayor of Bucharest, Nicușor Dan, won. Romania had escaped a populist takeover, but it was left with the EU's biggest budget deficit, over 9 percent of GDP, and someone had to fix it.

That job fell to Ilie Bolojan, a famously frugal former mayor who became prime minister in June 2025 at the head of a four-party pro-EU coalition. He raised taxes, froze public wages and pensions, and cut state jobs, bringing the deficit down from 9.3 to 7.9 percent ([GlobalPost](https://globalpost.com/stories/romanian-government-ousted-after-no-confidence-vote-by-socialist-and-far-right-alliance/)). The cuts tipped the economy into recession in the first quarter of 2026. They also hit the patronage networks of the Social Democrats (PSD), the coalition's largest party, whose voters were drifting to the far-right AUR. "Our members have become targets on the street because we are seen as the enablers of those who cut, close, and fire," said PSD leader Sorin Grindeanu ([Radio Moldova/Digi24](https://radiomoldova.md/p/74733)). The PSD quit on 23 April, then on 5 May joined the far right in a no-confidence vote that toppled Bolojan after ten months, even though polls showed him the coalition's most popular politician. The leu hit a record low. On 4 June President Dan nominated his adviser Eugen Tomac as prime minister ([wiiw](https://wiiw.ac.at/country-in-focus-romania-political-tug-of-war-places-economic-stability-in-danger-dlp-7649.pdf)). About €16 billion in EU recovery funds depended on reforms by 31 August.

**How power works:** A semi-presidential republic. The directly elected president, limited to two five-year terms, nominates the prime minister and leads on defence and foreign policy; the government needs the confidence of the bicameral parliament. The next parliamentary election is due in 2028.

**Who holds power:** President Nicușor Dan, since May 2025.

### Slovakia

In 2018 an investigative journalist, Ján Kuciak, and his fiancée were shot dead in their home while he was investigating links between politicians and organised crime. Mass protests forced Prime Minister Robert Fico to resign. He came back, won the 2023 election on a platform of ending military aid to Ukraine, and survived being shot five times by a lone gunman in 2024. Since then he has moved Slovakia toward Viktor Orbán's model, and in 2026 his allies in Europe got fewer.

Fico's government abolished the special prosecutor's office that handled high-level corruption, softened the criminal code in ways that let convicted fraudsters out early, and passed a law requiring NGOs with foreign funding to register, which critics called Russian-style. In 2026 parts of that agenda hit walls. Slovakia's Constitutional Court struck down the NGO law, and after the European Commission opened infringement proceedings in January, the coalition repealed a law dissolving the whistleblower protection office in March ([CIVICUS Monitor](https://monitor.civicus.org/explore/court-finds-russian-style-ngo-law-unconstitutional-controversial-whistleblower-reform-repealed-after-pressure/)). The European Parliament for the first time demanded proceedings to protect the EU budget from misuse in Slovakia, citing rural tourism subsidies diverted to luxury villas linked to Fico's associates ([Daniel Freund MEP](https://danielfreund.eu/slovakias-rule-of-law-under-attack-eu-parliament-demands-action/?lang=en)). In March Fico joined Orbán in refusing to back an EU loan for Ukraine. Then in April Orbán lost power in Hungary, leaving Fico as the EU's most Moscow-friendly leader. Tens of thousands have protested regularly since late 2024. The next election is due by 2027.

**How power works:** A parliamentary republic. The prime minister leads a coalition with the confidence of the 150-seat National Council; the directly elected president has limited powers but can veto laws and appoint judges.

**Who holds power:** Prime Minister Robert Fico (Smer), since October 2023; President Peter Pellegrini.

## Middle East

### Jordan

Jordan has survived by being useful to everyone. A desert kingdom with almost no oil and little water, carved out by Britain in 1921, it has absorbed waves of Palestinian, Iraqi and Syrian refugees, made peace with Israel in 1994, and hosted American forces while keeping a population that is majority Palestinian in origin and deeply hostile to Israel. King Abdullah II once described his country as stuck "between Iraq and a hard place." In 2026 it was between Iran and Israel, literally: missiles crossed its skies.

When the war began on 28 February, Iran targeted a US-used air base in eastern Jordan where a high-altitude air defence system is based and from which strikes on Iran were launched; Jordan's air force shot down Iranian missiles and drones over its territory ([AGBI](https://www.agbi.com/economy/2026/03/jordan-issues-two-month-moratorium-on-foreign-travel/)). Israeli gas imports, which keep Jordan's lights on, were suspended, and by late March the government had banned official foreign travel for two months to save money. Tourism, a pillar of the economy, took a hit as broadcasts showed missiles over Petra's country. Yet the war also raised Jordan's value: with Hormuz partly closed, its Red Sea port of Aqaba became a critical gateway for regional trade, and Iraq pressed ahead with an oil pipeline to it ([Middle East Institute](https://mei.edu/ar/publication/jordans-economy-weathers-iran-wars-shocks-and-finds-new-opportunities/)). At home, the king has kept tightening control. Jordan banned the Muslim Brotherhood in 2025 after security services found members making rockets and drones; in January the US designated the Jordanian branch a terrorist entity ([New America](https://www.newamerica.org/?p=60856)). Its political party, the largest bloc in parliament, has been told to cut ties and change its name.

**How power works:** A constitutional monarchy in which the king holds decisive power. He appoints the prime minister and the Senate and can dissolve the elected House of Representatives. The security services, especially the General Intelligence Directorate, are central to the state.

**Who holds power:** King Abdullah II, since 1999; Prime Minister Jafar Hassan.

### Kuwait

Kuwait was once the Gulf's experiment in democracy: since 1962 it has had the region's most powerful elected parliament, able to grill ministers and block budgets. Its citizens, about 1.5 million among a population of nearly five million, enjoy one of the world's most generous welfare states, funded by oil. In 2024 Emir Mishal al-Ahmad suspended parliament for up to four years, saying democracy was being abused. Since then he has been redefining who counts as Kuwaiti.

By 15 April 2026, more than 71,000 people had been stripped of citizenship, about 4.6 percent of all citizens, according to the Newsroom Initiative, which tracks official gazette decrees ([New Lines](https://newlinesmag.com/essays/kuwait-is-stripping-its-people-of-citizenship-at-an-unprecedented-rate/)). At least 26,000 of those affected are women who gained citizenship through marriage; others include former soldiers, doctors, athletes and artists. The government says it is correcting fraudulent naturalisations. A new decree-law in April restricted citizenship by origin to families present before 1920 and made nationality decisions "sovereign acts" beyond judicial review ([bne IntelliNews](https://www.intellinews.com/kuwait-citizenship-purge-tops-50-000-as-new-decree-tightens-revocation-rules-437951/)). On 3 July further amendments abolished naturalisation altogether ([ADHRB](https://www.adhrb.org/2026/07/kuwaits-citizenship-law-overhaul-amid-mass-revocations-and-growing-human-rights-concerns/)). The Iran war briefly paused the announcements, then they resumed within days of the April ceasefire, and in September another 415 people lost their nationality. The war also cut Kuwait's oil exports, which must pass through Hormuz, forcing its state oil company to declare force majeure. Critics who speak out have been summoned by state security.

**How power works:** A hereditary emirate ruled by the Al Sabah family. The emir appoints the crown prince and prime minister; the elected 50-seat National Assembly, normally one of the Gulf's strongest, has been suspended since May 2024, and the emir rules by decree.

**Who holds power:** Emir Mishal al-Ahmad al-Jaber Al Sabah, since December 2023; Prime Minister Ahmad Abdullah al-Sabah.

### Qatar

In 1971 Qatar was a small, poor pearl-diving peninsula. Then it started exporting the gas from the North Field, the world's largest, which it shares with Iran under the sea, where Iran calls it South Pars. Qatar became, per person, one of the richest countries on Earth, supplier of about a fifth of the world's liquefied natural gas. It built a reputation as everyone's mediator, hosting Hamas's political office and the US military's largest regional base at the same time. In 2026 the war put both its gas and its neutrality under fire.

Iranian drones struck the giant LNG complex at Ras Laffan and the Mesaieed industrial city on 2 March, and QatarEnergy halted production and declared force majeure two days later; with Hormuz effectively closed, it could not ship anyway ([Reuters via Pipeline & Gas Journal](https://pgjonline.com/news/2026/march/qatarenergy-halts-lng-output-declares-force-majeure-after-facility-attacks)). On 18 March, after Israel bombed the Iranian side of the shared field, Iranian missiles caused "extensive damage" at Ras Laffan. QatarEnergy's chief said the strike wiped out about 17 percent of export capacity, roughly $20 billion a year in lost revenue, and the company declared force majeure for up to five years on contracts with buyers in Italy, Belgium, South Korea and China ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/3/24/qatarenergy-declares-force-majeure-on-some-lng-contracts)). European gas prices nearly doubled. Qatar called the attack "blatant aggression" and expelled Iranian officials, a break with years of careful balance with Tehran. Pakistan, not Qatar, became the main US-Iran go-between, though Qatari envoys still shuttled to Tehran.

**How power works:** An absolute hereditary monarchy ruled by the Al Thani family. The emir appoints the prime minister and cabinet; the Shura Council is advisory and, since 2024, fully appointed. Citizens are about 10 percent of the population.

**Who holds power:** Emir Tamim bin Hamad Al Thani, since 2013; Prime Minister Mohammed bin Abdulrahman Al Thani.
