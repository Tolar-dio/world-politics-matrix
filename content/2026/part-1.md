This is Part 1 rewritten in the new style: each entry opens with the history that gives this year's events their weight, then the year, then how power works. All 30 entries were re-sourced and updated to 12 October, and several stories have moved since the original: Brazil's first round, Morocco's first woman head of government, Spain's snap election, Sweden's failed government formation and Kosovo's last-minute president.

The through-line of this batch is a single question asked in dozens of places: who gets to stay? In Uganda, Djibouti and Congo, men who have ruled for decades stayed by rewriting the rules. In Hungary, Bangladesh and Nepal, voters or protesters threw entrenched rulers out. In Iran and the US-Venezuela story, outside force did it instead, and the results look less like change than anyone expected.

## Africa

### Benin

In 1991, Benin became the first country in mainland Africa where a sitting ruler lost an election and handed over power peacefully. For a generation it was held up as proof that the continent's "Big Man" era could end. In 2026 it held another orderly transfer of power, and almost nobody outside the government treated it as a celebration.

President Patrice Talon stepped down after two terms, as the constitution requires. His handpicked successor, finance minister Romuald Wadagni, won the 12 April election with 94.05 percent. His only opponent took under 6 percent because the main opposition party, Les Démocrates, was kept off the ballot ([KAS](https://www.kas.de/en/country-reports/detail/-/content/praesidentschaftswahl-in-benin-im-kontext-demokratischen-rueckschritts-in-westafrika)). In January's parliamentary vote the same party fell short of a 20 percent threshold, leaving Talon's coalition with every seat ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/amp/news/2026/4/13/government-candidate-wadagni-on-course-to-win-benin-presidential-vote)). The vote came four months after a coup attempt in December 2025 that was put down with help from Nigerian troops, and with jihadists of JNIM pressing into the north, where they killed 54 soldiers at one post in 2025. Talon's defenders point to roads, ports and fiscal discipline; his critics point to rules rewritten since 2016 so that his camp could not lose. Benin shows that democracy can erode without a coup: through thresholds, candidate rules and an election whose result everyone knew in advance.

**How power works:** A presidential republic. The president is directly elected for five years with a two-term limit and heads the government; the National Assembly is elected by proportional representation with high party thresholds.

**Who holds power:** President Romuald Wadagni, Talon's successor, in office since May 2026.

### Djibouti

Djibouti is a country of about a million people that matters far more than its size suggests, because it sits on the Bab el-Mandeb, the narrow strait where the Red Sea meets the Gulf of Aden and much of Europe-Asia trade passes. The US, China, France and Japan all have military bases there, and the rent, along with Ethiopia's port traffic, keeps the economy running. That strategic value has also helped one family keep running the country.

On 10 April, President Ismaïl Omar Guelleh, 78, won a sixth term with 97.81 percent against a single challenger, a former ruling-party member ([AP via WSLS](https://www.wsls.com/news/world/2026/04/11/djibouti-president-ismail-omar-guelleh-is-reelected-for-a-sixth-term/)). He had promised his fifth term would be his last; then, in October 2025, parliament unanimously scrapped the 75-year age limit that stood in his way. The two main opposition parties have boycotted every election since 2016, after he abolished term limits in 2010 ([International IDEA](https://www.idea.int/democracytracker/report/djibouti/april-2026)). Guelleh succeeded his uncle, Djibouti's first president, in 1999. Foreign powers that depend on their bases there have said little.

**How power works:** A presidential republic in form, a dominant-party state in practice. The president is directly elected for five years with no term limit and appoints the prime minister and government; the ruling coalition dominates the National Assembly.

**Who holds power:** President Ismaïl Omar Guelleh, in office since 1999.

### Ethiopia

In 2019, Abiy Ahmed won the Nobel Peace Prize for ending a 20-year standoff with Eritrea. A year later he went to war in Tigray, a conflict that killed hundreds of thousands, with Eritrean troops fighting on his side. In 2026 he won another election, and the former partners in that war were edging toward a new one against each other.

Abiy's Prosperity Party won 438 seats in the 1 June parliamentary election, nearly 90 percent, amid what AP called a crackdown on dissent, with his most prominent critics jailed, exiled or missing ([AP via WSPA](https://www.wspa.com/news/world-news/ap-international/ap-ethiopias-ruling-party-retains-parliamentary-majority-in-election-marred-by-insecurity/amp/)). Some 143 polling stations never opened because of fighting in Amhara and Oromia, where insurgencies continue ([AFP via Africanews](https://www.africanews.com/amp/2026/06/22/ethiopian-prime-ministers-party-sweeps-parliamentary-vote/)). In Tigray, the TPLF restored its pre-war administration just before the vote, pushing aside an interim government Abiy had appointed. Abiy has also repeatedly said landlocked Ethiopia, with 130 million people, must regain access to the Red Sea, which it lost when Eritrea became independent in 1993; Eritrea calls that imperial ambition and Addis Ababa accuses Asmara of arming rebels ([Rainbow Radio](https://rainbowradioonline.com/2026/06/22/ethiopia-pms-party-wins-landslide-as-fears-grow-of-new-conflict/)). The economy grows fast on construction and coffee, yet 43 percent of Ethiopians live in poverty. Parliament is due to re-elect Abiy this month.

**How power works:** A federal parliamentary republic of ethnically defined regions. The prime minister, chosen by the House of Peoples' Representatives, holds executive power; the president is ceremonial. In practice the Prosperity Party dominates.

**Who holds power:** Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed (Prosperity Party), in office since 2018.

### Morocco

In September 2025, eight pregnant women died at a public hospital in Agadir. The deaths set off Morocco's largest protests in years, led by a leaderless online movement calling itself GenZ 212, whose chant, "We want hospitals, not just stadiums," hit at a government spending billions on the 2030 World Cup. The protesters appealed directly to King Mohammed VI and demanded that Prime Minister Aziz Akhannouch, a billionaire businessman, resign ([KAS](https://www.kas.de/de/laenderberichte/detail/-/content/marokkos-jugend-vor-der-wahl-2026-1)). A year later, Moroccans got their answer at the ballot box, in a way.

In the 23 September election, Akhannouch's RNI fell to 66 seats. Its coalition partner, the Party of Authenticity and Modernity (PAM), came first with 97, boosted by recruiting Fouzi Lekjaa, the head of the football federation, weeks before the vote ([Reuters via TimesLive](https://www.timeslive.co.za/news/africa/2026-09-29-moroccos-king-appoints-first-woman-prime-minister-following-election/)). Turnout was just 38 percent, the lowest since 2007, a sign that many young people still do not see elections as the place where change happens ([Kuwait Times](https://kuwaittimes.com/article/50380/world/africa/moroccos-ruling-coalition-tops-parliament-vote/)). On 29 September, the king appointed PAM's Fatima Ezzahra El Mansouri, a lawyer and former Marrakech mayor, as Morocco's first woman head of government. Her government will oversee the last stretch of a $20 billion World Cup build-out, the same spending the protesters questioned.

**How power works:** A constitutional monarchy where the king holds most real power, controlling defence, foreign affairs, religion and security. He must appoint the head of government from the party that wins most seats in the 395-seat House of Representatives, and the government handles domestic policy.

**Who holds power:** King Mohammed VI; Head of Government Fatima Ezzahra El Mansouri (PAM), appointed September 2026.

### Republic of Congo

Denis Sassou Nguesso first took power in 1979. He lost it in 1992 in the country's first multiparty election, won it back by force in a 1997 civil war, and has held it since. On 15 March 2026, at 82, he won a fifth consecutive term with 94.82 percent ([AP via Africanews](https://www.africanews.com/amp/2026/03/17/republic-of-congos-president-reelected-to-fifth-term-provisional-results-show/)).

The election had the familiar features of an outcome settled in advance. The government shut down the internet and SMS messaging nationwide. Before election day, Sassou was the only candidate allowed to campaign across the country, some opposition figures were in prison and others boycotted ([African Elections Project](https://africanelections.org/elections/democratic-republic-of-the-congo/2026/)). Brazzaville's streets were lined with his portraits, but many polling stations had short or no queues, with residents saying there was no point. A 2015 referendum removed the age and term limits that would have stopped him. Congo is an oil producer whose wealth has not reached most of its 6 million people, and much of its debt is owed to China and oil traders. Sassou is now Africa's third-longest-serving ruler, behind only Equatorial Guinea's Obiang and Cameroon's Biya, part of a club of octogenarians who keep winning.

**How power works:** A presidential republic with a dominant executive. The president is directly elected for five years with no term limit and appoints the prime minister and government; the Congolese Party of Labour dominates parliament.

**Who holds power:** President Denis Sassou Nguesso, in power since 1997 and earlier from 1979 to 1992.

### Uganda

Yoweri Museveni took power in 1986 after a five-year bush war, promising that the problem of Africa was leaders who overstayed. Forty years later, at 81, he won a seventh term.

The 15 January election gave him 71.65 percent, his strongest result in 30 years, against 24.72 percent for Robert Kyagulanyi, the singer-turned-politician known as Bobi Wine ([GlobalSecurity](https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/world/uganda/politics-2026.htm)). It was held under a nationwide internet blackout. During the campaign security forces fired tear gas and bullets at Wine's rallies; in a New Year's Eve speech Museveni urged police to use more tear gas on "the criminal opposition," saying it "doesn't kill." On election day soldiers raided Wine's home. He said he escaped and went into hiding, and at least seven people died in post-election clashes ([Reuters via AllAfrica](https://allafrica.com/stories/202601190391.html)). Wine called the result fraud. The bigger question in Uganda is what comes after Museveni: his son, General Muhoozi Kainerugaba, the army chief known for erratic social media posts, is widely seen as being prepared to succeed him. Uganda also has troops in South Sudan backing President Kiir.

**How power works:** A presidential republic. The president is directly elected for five years; term and age limits were removed in 2005 and 2017. The ruling National Resistance Movement dominates parliament, and the army is closely tied to the presidency.

**Who holds power:** President Yoweri Museveni (NRM), in power since 1986.

## Americas

### Brazil

In 2022, Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva beat Jair Bolsonaro by less than two points. A week after Lula took office, Bolsonaro's supporters stormed Congress, the Supreme Court and the presidential palace in Brasília, and in 2025 Bolsonaro was convicted of plotting a coup and sentenced to 27 years in prison. That is the backdrop for 2026: Brazil is choosing between the same two camps, with the father replaced by his son.

On 4 October, Senator Flávio Bolsonaro, 45, Jair's eldest son, beat the polls and finished first with 47.45 percent; Lula, now 80, took 44.66 percent ([Reuters via Emirates 24/7](https://www.emirates247.com/world/flavio-bolsonaro-outperforms-polls-advances-to-brazil-presidential-runoff-against-lula/6341)). Nearly every pre-election survey had Lula ahead in the first round. Lula admitted he had expected to win outright; Flávio called the result "the beginning of the end" of the Workers' Party era and quickly picked up endorsements from defeated right-wing governors Ronaldo Caiado and Romeu Zema ([Blueprint](https://blueprint.ng/?p=850851)). The right also did well in congressional races, which could give Flávio a working base if he wins ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/5/brazils-presidential-race-three-key-takeaways-from-the-first-round)). Lula has accused Trump of trying to tilt the race. Voters' top worries are crime, corruption and the cost of living. The runoff is on 25 October, and the question is whether Brazil hands power back to the family whose last term ended with a mob in the capital.

**How power works:** A federal presidential republic. The president is directly elected for four years with one consecutive re-election, needing a majority, with a runoff if no one passes 50 percent. Congress has two chambers and is highly fragmented, so presidents govern through coalitions with dozens of parties; the 26 states and federal district have their own governors.

**Who holds power:** President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva (PT), in office since 2023, facing a runoff on 25 October.

### Colombia

In 2022, Colombia elected its first left-wing president, the former guerrilla Gustavo Petro, who promised "total peace" through talks with every armed group in the country. Four years later, with those talks largely failed and violence rising, Colombians swung hard the other way.

Abelardo de la Espriella, a 47-year-old millionaire criminal defence lawyer with his own clothing and rum brands, who has never held office, campaigned as "The Tiger" on a Bukele-style plan to fight armed groups and build ten mega-prisons. Endorsed by Trump, he won the 21 June runoff with 49.66 percent to 48.70 percent for Iván Cepeda, Petro's chosen successor, a margin of about 250,000 votes out of nearly 26 million ([Edelman](https://www.edelman.com/insights/colombia-2026-2030-presidential-elections-results-first-100-days)). The result was a clear rejection of Petro's government; Cepeda conceded after a recount and took the Senate seat reserved for the runner-up ([AP via KOB](https://www.kob.com/?p=1091935)). It was also a historic first: no far-right candidate had won Colombia's presidency in its modern history, while Cepeda's 12.7 million votes were the most ever for the left ([Finance Colombia](https://www.financecolombia.com/?id=25319)). De la Espriella, who once represented Maduro associate Alex Saab, took office on 7 August. A country nearly split in half now waits to see whether a hard line brings the security that dialogue did not.

**How power works:** A presidential republic. The president is directly elected for a single four-year term, with re-election banned, and holds strong executive powers; Congress has a Senate and a House of Representatives.

**Who holds power:** President Abelardo de la Espriella (Defensores de la Patria), in office since 7 August 2026.

### Costa Rica

Costa Rica abolished its army in 1948 and built a reputation as Central America's peaceful, green, stable democracy. That image has been cracking. Cocaine trafficking through its Pacific ports has pushed murders to record highs, and in 2026 voters chose the candidate promising to govern like the man who had broken its political habits.

On 1 February, Laura Fernández, a 39-year-old political scientist who had served as a minister under the outgoing president, won in the first round with about 48 percent, more than enough to avoid a runoff, against 33 percent for the traditional National Liberation Party ([AP via Ottumwa Courier](https://www.ottumwacourier.com/news/national_news/costa-rica-election/image_755b8f7d-87ea-5297-b1e2-97015c58cd57.html)). She campaigned as the openly declared "heir" of President Rodrigo Chaves, a combative populist barred from re-election, and supporters chanted "Long live Chaves!" at her rallies ([Confidencial](https://confidencial.digital/en/?p=182788)). She is only the second woman to lead Costa Rica, after Laura Chinchilla. Her Pueblo Soberano party became the largest bloc in congress, with about 31 of 57 seats, the biggest governing caucus since 1982, but short of the supermajority Chaves wanted in order to name Supreme Court judges ([NPR via WGCU](https://www.wgcu.org/2026-02-02/populist-conservative-laura-fernandez-wins-costa-ricas-presidential-election)). Turnout jumped to 69 percent. Costa Rica's institutions are now testing whether they can absorb a populist movement without bending.

**How power works:** A presidential republic. The president is directly elected for four years, needing 40 percent to avoid a runoff, and cannot serve consecutive terms. The 57-seat Legislative Assembly is unicameral. There is no army.

**Who holds power:** President Laura Fernández (Pueblo Soberano), in office since 8 May 2026.

### Peru

Peru has had nine presidents in ten years. They have been impeached, jailed, forced out by Congress or have resigned in disgrace, and in 2022-23 protests against the political class left about 50 demonstrators dead. In 2026, Peruvians gave the job to the most familiar name in their politics.

Keiko Fujimori, daughter of Alberto Fujimori, the 1990s president who defeated the Shining Path insurgency and then turned authoritarian before being jailed for human rights crimes, finally won on her fourth try. After a first round in which she took just 17 percent among 35 candidates, she beat leftist congressman Roberto Sánchez in the 7 June runoff with 50.135 percent, a margin of 49,641 votes out of 18 million ([MercoPress](https://en.mercopress.com/2026/06/30/keiko-fujimori-wins-peru-s-presidency-as-the-full-count-confirms-her-by-49-641-votes/comments)). Sánchez initially refused to recognise the result. Fujimori campaigned on crime, especially the extortion gangs that have terrorised bus drivers and small shopkeepers, promising four new prisons plus one modelled on El Salvador's CECOT mega-prison, with inmates forced to work ([AP via Las Vegas Sun](https://lasvegassun.com/news/2026/jul/03/conservative-candidate-keiko-fujimori-wins-perus-p/)). She took office in July. Whether she lasts a full term, in a country where Congress routinely removes presidents, is the real question.

**How power works:** A presidential republic with unusually strong congressional checks: the unicameral Congress can remove a president for "moral incapacity," a vague clause it has used repeatedly. The president is directly elected for five years with a runoff and cannot serve consecutive terms. A new Senate returns in 2026.

**Who holds power:** President Keiko Fujimori (Fuerza Popular), in office since July 2026.

### United States

In the early hours of 3 January, US forces raided Caracas and carried Nicolás Maduro to New York to face drug charges. On 28 February, the US and Israel went to war with Iran and killed its supreme leader. In between and since, the administration imposed 50 percent tariffs on Canada, its closest ally, and hosted the World Cup. Few years have shown so plainly how much one American president can reshape the world without asking Congress first. Americans now get their first chance to answer at the ballot box.

The midterm elections are on 3 November, and the mood is bleak for the president's party. An NPR/PBS/Marist poll in September put Trump's approval at 39 percent, with 53 percent strongly disapproving, the highest share since the question was first asked in 2017; 45 percent of voters said their vote for Congress was a vote against him ([NPR via WGCU](https://www.wgcu.org/2026-09-23/poll-most-voters-agree-the-2026-midterms-are-all-about-trump-and-they-disapprove)). A Reuters/Ipsos poll this month put him at 32 percent, and he has been campaigning in normally safe Republican areas of Texas ([ABC News](https://www.goodmorningamerica.com/video/137077623)). Trump told Republicans at an unprecedented midterm convention in Dallas to "pretend that I'm on the ballot," while some Republican candidates have begun breaking with him on key issues. If Democrats take the House, the second half of Trump's term will look very different: subpoenas, blocked bills and possibly impeachment.

**How power works:** A federal presidential republic. The president is elected indirectly through the Electoral College for four years, with a two-term limit. Congress has a 100-member Senate and a 435-member House, all of whose seats and a third of the Senate's are up every two years. The Supreme Court and the 50 states share power under the Constitution.

**Who holds power:** President Donald Trump (Republican); Republicans control both chambers of Congress until at least January 2027.

### Venezuela

For more than a decade Venezuela's opposition, backed by Washington, tried to remove Nicolás Maduro through elections, protests and sanctions, as some 8 million people fled the country. In January the US finally removed him, by force. What followed surprised almost everyone: Washington chose to work not with the opposition but with Maduro's own vice president.

After the 3 January raid, a court installed Delcy Rodríguez as acting president, and the Trump administration recognised her as head of state, lifted sanctions on her personally on 1 April, then on the central bank, and sent officials to Caracas to discuss access to Venezuela's oil and minerals ([AP via Herald Extra](https://www.heraldextra.com/top-headlines/2026/apr/01/us-lifts-sanctions-on-venezuelas-acting-president-delcy-rodriguez/)). Maduro has pleaded not guilty in New York and legally remains president. In March, Venezuela even beat the US to win the World Baseball Classic, and Rodríguez posed with the trophy at the presidential palace. Opposition leader María Corina Machado, the 2025 Nobel Peace laureate, held rallies abroad as her movement was sidelined. On 23 September, in her first UN General Assembly speech, Rodríguez promised that "elections will be held in Venezuela," but at the "right moment" decided by Venezuelans, with no date; she met Trump the night before ([NPR via KAXE](https://www.kaxe.org/news/2026-09-24/venezuelas-acting-leader-promises-elections-at-the-right-moment)). Venezuela changed hands without changing regimes.

**How power works:** Formally a presidential republic; in practice the ruling PSUV controls all institutions. Rodríguez governs as acting president under a court order, with the National Assembly led by her brother Jorge. The military remains a central power broker.

**Who holds power:** Acting President Delcy Rodríguez (PSUV), since January 2026.

## Asia

### Bangladesh

For three decades, Bangladesh's politics was a feud between two women: Sheikh Hasina, daughter of the country's founding father, and Khaleda Zia, widow of the general who ruled after him. They took turns in power and took turns jailing each other's people. In August 2024, student-led protests that left hundreds dead toppled Hasina, who fled by helicopter to India. In 2026, Bangladesh held its first election since, and the winner was Khaleda Zia's son.

On 12 February, Tarique Rahman's Bangladesh Nationalist Party won a landslide, and he was sworn in as prime minister on 17 February, taking over from Nobel laureate Muhammad Yunus, who had led an interim government for 18 months ([People Daily](https://peopledaily.digital/inside-politics/bangladeshs-incoming-pm-tarique-rahman-sworn-into-parliament/amp)). Rahman, 60, had returned only in December after 17 years of exile in Britain, fleeing multiple convictions under Hasina, including a life sentence, that his supporters called political ([CIDOB](https://www.cidob.org/en/lider-politico/tarique-rahman)). Hasina's Awami League was barred from running, and Hasina herself has been sentenced to death in absentia for crimes against humanity over the crackdown on the protests. The Islamist Jamaat-e-Islami, crushed under Hasina, made big gains and conceded as the main opposition. Rahman has promised to end disappearances and extrajudicial killings, but Human Rights Watch notes the notorious Rapid Action Battalion survives under a new name, mob violence has surged and attacks on Hindus and other minorities continue ([HRW](https://www.hrw.org/asia/bangladesh.php)). The deeper question for 170 million Bangladeshis is whether the revolution of 2024 ends the old feud or just hands it to the next generation.

**How power works:** A parliamentary republic. The prime minister leads the majority in the 350-seat Jatiya Sangsad (300 directly elected, 50 reserved for women); the president, elected by parliament, is largely ceremonial.

**Who holds power:** Prime Minister Tarique Rahman (BNP), in office since 17 February 2026.

### Japan

Japan went through five prime ministers in five years, each weaker than the last, while the long-ruling Liberal Democratic Party lost its majority in 2024 amid a slush-fund scandal. Then in October 2025 the party chose Sanae Takaichi, a hard-line conservative who admires Margaret Thatcher, as Japan's first woman prime minister. Within weeks she said Japan could respond militarily if China attacked Taiwan, the most direct statement by any sitting Japanese leader. China was furious and hit back where it hurt: it told its citizens not to travel to Japan, restricted seafood imports and even took back Japan's last two pandas ([AFP via Edition.mv](https://edition.mv/world/48163)).

Takaichi bet that voters would side with her. She called a snap election in the middle of winter, only the third February vote since the war, and on 8 February the LDP won 315 of 465 seats, a two-thirds supermajority on its own for the first time in its 70-year history ([Stanford APARC](https://aparc.fsi.stanford.edu/node/38945)). The victory gives her the numbers to push higher military spending, a $135 billion stimulus and tax cuts that have rattled bond markets worried about Japan's enormous debt, and possibly to revise the pacifist constitution. Analysts read the result as voters endorsing her stand against Beijing's pressure. Japan has gone from a revolving door to one of the strongest governments among the rich democracies, led by a nationalist.

**How power works:** A constitutional monarchy with a parliamentary system. The emperor is symbolic; the prime minister is chosen by the Diet, mainly the 465-seat House of Representatives, and leads the cabinet. The upper House of Councillors can delay legislation, and a two-thirds vote in both houses plus a referendum is needed to amend the constitution.

**Who holds power:** Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi (LDP), in office since October 2025.

### Myanmar

On 1 February 2021, the day Myanmar's newly elected parliament was to sit, the army arrested Aung San Suu Kyi, whose party had won 396 of 476 seats, and seized power, claiming fraud. The coup set off a civil war that has killed tens of thousands and displaced millions. Five years later, the general who led it found a way to make his rule look civilian.

After a three-phase election in December and January organised by the junta and dismissed by observers as neither free nor fair, the army-backed Union Solidarity and Development Party dominated a parliament where a quarter of seats are reserved for soldiers. On 3 April, it elected Senior General Min Aung Hlaing president with 429 of 584 votes ([AFP via NST](https://www.nst.com.my/amp/world/region/2026/04/1410016/myanmar-junta-chief-elected-president-pro-military-mps)). The vote took place in a parliament building in Naypyidaw repaired after the 2025 earthquake. Because the constitution forbids a president from also leading the army, he handed command on 30 March to Ye Win Oo, a former intelligence chief seen as fiercely loyal ([Reuters via Chronicle.lu](https://chronicle.lu/category/international-news/60465-myanmar-junta-chief-min-aung-hlaing-elected-president-by-pro-military-parliament)). China, his key ally, promptly congratulated him. Suu Kyi, now 81, remains in prison. Analysts expect the new government to court neighbours for recognition while pressing the war against ethnic armies and resistance forces that still hold large parts of the country.

**How power works:** A presidential system under the military-written 2008 constitution. Parliament elects the president; the military holds 25 percent of seats, a veto over constitutional change, and control of the defence, interior and border ministries.

**Who holds power:** President Min Aung Hlaing, with the army under Commander-in-Chief Ye Win Oo.

### Nepal

In September 2025, Nepal's government banned two dozen social media platforms. Young people, already furious at corruption and at politicians' children flaunting luxury on Instagram, poured into the streets. Police opened fire; parliament and politicians' homes were set ablaze, more than 70 people died, and Prime Minister K.P. Sharma Oli resigned. Six months later, the generation that brought him down picked his replacement.

In the 5 March election, the Rastriya Swatantra Party, founded only in 2022, won 182 of 275 seats, and its candidate for prime minister was Balendra "Balen" Shah, a 35-year-old structural engineer and former underground rapper whose lyrics attacked corruption, and who was elected mayor of Kathmandu as an independent in 2022 ([Drishti IAS](https://www.drishtiias.com/state-pcs-current-affairs/balen-shah-becomes-nepals-youngest-prime-minister/print_manually)). He chose to run against Oli himself in Oli's home seat, Jhapa-5, and beat him by 49,614 votes, the largest margin in Nepal's parliamentary history ([Asia News Network](https://asianews.network/?p=261300)). Sworn in in late March, Balen became the youngest prime minister in Nepal's history and its first from the Madhesi community of the southern plains, long marginalised by hill elites. The challenges are immediate: the RSP has promised 7 percent annual growth in one of Asia's poorer countries, his party chief faces legal charges, and critics have already questioned the luxury Land Rover he campaigned in ([ISAS](https://www.isas.nus.edu.sg/?p=18864)). Nepal is now testing whether a protest movement can govern.

**How power works:** A federal parliamentary republic since the monarchy was abolished in 2008. The prime minister leads the majority in the 275-seat House of Representatives, elected by a mix of constituencies and proportional lists; the president is largely ceremonial.

**Who holds power:** Prime Minister Balendra Shah (RSP), in office since March 2026.

### Thailand

For 20 years Thai politics has been a fight between the conservative establishment of army, palace and courts and the parties of Thaksin Shinawatra and, later, young reformers. The establishment usually lost elections and won anyway, by coup or court ruling. In 2026, for the first time in decades, a party of the establishment simply won.

Anutin Charnvirakul, who became prime minister in September 2025 after a court removed Thaksin's daughter Paetongtarn over a leaked phone call with Cambodia's Hun Sen, called a snap election during the border war with Cambodia. On 8 February his Bhumjaithai party, known before mainly for legalising cannabis, won 192 of 500 seats, up from 71 ([Reuters via NST](https://www.nst.com.my/amp/world/region/2026/02/1374234/thailands-bhumjaithai-prepares-coalition-talks-after-big-election-win)). The progressive People's Party, which had led every poll, fell to about 117, and Thaksin's Pheu Thai had its worst result ever ([Malay Mail](https://www.malaymail.com/news/world/2026/02/25/thailand-election-body-confirms-win-by-pms-party/210438)). Analysts credited nationalism over the Cambodia clashes, defections of rural MPs to Bhumjaithai, and the People's Party's damaging decision to prop Anutin up in office in 2025 ([Asialink](https://asialink.unimelb.edu.au/diplomacy/insights/why-bhumjaithai-won/)). Anutin formed a coalition with Pheu Thai and was re-elected prime minister on 19 March. The young urban voters who powered the reformist wave of 2023 are now in opposition, and the promised rewrite of the military-era constitution looks less urgent to those in power.

**How power works:** A constitutional monarchy with a parliamentary system. The prime minister needs a majority in the 500-seat House of Representatives; the king is revered and protected by strict lèse-majesté laws, and the courts and military have repeatedly removed elected governments.

**Who holds power:** Prime Minister Anutin Charnvirakul (Bhumjaithai), re-elected March 2026; King Vajiralongkorn.

## Europe

### Armenia

In 2023, Azerbaijan took Nagorno-Karabakh in a lightning offensive and more than 100,000 ethnic Armenians fled a land their people had lived in for centuries. Russia, Armenia's treaty ally, whose peacekeepers were on the ground, did nothing. Since then, Prime Minister Nikol Pashinyan, a former journalist who came to power in the peaceful 2018 Velvet Revolution, has bet his country's future on making peace with Azerbaijan and Turkey and turning toward the West. In June 2026, Armenians voted on that bet.

On 7 June, Pashinyan's Civil Contract won 49.8 percent, enough to govern alone for a third term ([AP via KTLA](https://ktla.com/news/nationworld/ap-international/ap-armenian-prime-minister-nikol-pashinyan-claims-victory-following-general-election/amp/)). The runner-up, with 23.3 percent, was Strong Armenia, led by Samvel Karapetyan, a billionaire who made his fortune in Russia and spent the campaign under house arrest, accused of calling for the government's overthrow, a charge he calls political. Former president Robert Kocharyan's bloc took about 10 percent. Pashinyan fell short of the two-thirds he needs to change the constitution, which matters because Azerbaijan says it will not sign the peace treaty until Armenia drops constitutional language it sees as a claim on Karabakh ([EVN Report](https://evnreport.com/?p=46426)). Trump endorsed Pashinyan before the vote. Moscow has answered Armenia's westward turn by banning imports of Armenian fruit, vegetables and milk. Analysts credited the win to the peace process and normalisation with Turkey, but also to pressure on public employees to back the ruling party ([PISM](https://pism.pl/publications/pashinyans-party-wins-parliamentary-elections)).

**How power works:** A parliamentary republic since 2018. The prime minister leads the majority in the National Assembly, elected by proportional representation with a bonus to guarantee a stable majority; the president, elected by parliament, is ceremonial.

**Who holds power:** Prime Minister Nikol Pashinyan (Civil Contract), in office since 2018.

### Bulgaria

Bulgaria is the poorest country in the EU, and between 2021 and 2026 it held eight parliamentary elections, none producing a government that lasted more than about a year. In December 2025, some 100,000 people, many of them young, filled the square outside parliament in Sofia to protest a budget and a political class they saw as captured by oligarchs. The government fell. On 1 January, Bulgaria adopted the euro anyway, becoming the eurozone's 21st member with no budget in place.

Into that vacuum stepped Rumen Radev, a former fighter pilot who had been the largely ceremonial president since 2017. He resigned the presidency in January, founded Progressive Bulgaria in March, and on 19 April won 44.7 percent and 131 of 240 seats, the first single-party majority since 1997 ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/amp/news/2026/4/19/exit-poll-shows-former-president-radevs-party-set-to-win-bulgaria-election)). Boyko Borissov's GERB, which had dominated Bulgarian politics for 15 years, fell to about 13 percent. Parliament approved Radev as prime minister on 8 May by 124 votes to 70 ([AP via WTOP](https://wtop.com/news/2026/05/bulgarias-parliament-votes-new-government-into-office/)). He campaigned on dismantling the "mafia state" and lowering prices, criticised the euro switch and is seen as friendlier to Moscow than his predecessors, though analysts expect him to stay moderate because Bulgaria depends heavily on EU money. Critics noted his party later voted against measures targeting Delyan Peevski, the sanctioned oligarch he had campaigned against. A presidential election is due in November.

**How power works:** A parliamentary republic. The prime minister leads the majority in the 240-seat National Assembly; the directly elected president is largely ceremonial but appoints caretaker governments when parliament deadlocks, which is why the office mattered so much in recent years.

**Who holds power:** Prime Minister Rumen Radev (Progressive Bulgaria), in office since May 2026.

### Denmark

In January 2026, the president of the United States renewed his push to take Greenland, a semi-autonomous territory of the Danish kingdom, and refused to rule out force. Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen warned that an American takeover would mean the end of NATO, and Europe rallied behind her ([Euronews](https://euronews.com/2026/03/24/denmark-votes-in-snap-election-as-mette-frederiksen-seeks-third-term)). It was the most dramatic moment of her career, and she tried to turn it into a third term.

Frederiksen called a snap election for 24 March, months early. It backfired. Greenland barely featured, because Danes broadly agree on it, and the campaign turned on prices and welfare instead. Her Social Democrats had their worst result in 120 years, and her left-leaning bloc won 84 seats, short of the 90 needed for a majority ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/3/24/polls-open-in-key-denmark-elections-clouded-by-trumps-greenland-threat)). Her hard line on immigration had alienated left-wing voters without winning over the right. The kingmaker became Lars Løkke Rasmussen, her foreign minister and a former prime minister, whose centrist Moderates could swing either way. After weeks of talks, Frederiksen formed her third cabinet in early June. The four seats held by Greenland and the Faroe Islands, normally afterthoughts, briefly looked like they could decide who governed.

**How power works:** A constitutional monarchy with a parliamentary system. The prime minister needs the confidence of the 179-seat Folketing, including two members each from Greenland and the Faroe Islands; minority governments relying on deals across blocs are common. King Frederik X is head of state.

**Who holds power:** Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen (Social Democrats), in her third government since June 2026.

### Hungary

For 16 years Viktor Orbán ran Hungary as the EU's self-declared "illiberal democracy": he rewrote the constitution, redrew electoral maps, filled courts with loyalists and turned state television into a party outlet, while blocking EU policy on Ukraine and cultivating Putin and Trump. Many people believed he had rigged the system so thoroughly that he could not lose. On 12 April, he lost in a landslide.

Péter Magyar, a former Fidesz insider who broke with Orbán in 2024, led his new Tisza party to 141 of 199 seats, the largest majority in Hungary's post-communist history, while Fidesz fell from 135 to 52 ([AP via KTLA](https://ktla.com/news/nationworld/ap-international/ap-hungarys-magyar-announces-ministers-after-landslide-election-win/amp/)). Orbán conceded on election night. Magyar was sworn in on 9 May promising to "serve, not rule" and a "change of system" ([The Star](https://www.the-star.co.ke/news/world/2026-05-09-i-will-serve-not-rule-over-hungary-says-new-pm)). With a two-thirds supermajority, he can now rewrite Orbán's constitution. He suspended the news programming of the state broadcaster, which had served Fidesz for years, until it could be independent, created separate ministries for health, education and environment, and set out to unlock about €17 billion in EU funds frozen over rule-of-law concerns. Critics pounced when his brother-in-law was named justice minister. His first real crisis was a record heatwave and drought. He has said Hungary still needs Russian oil and gas in the short term. Orbán's defeat removed the EU's biggest internal obstacle and the model that populists across Europe had studied.

**How power works:** A parliamentary republic. The prime minister leads the majority in the 199-seat National Assembly, elected by a mixed system; a two-thirds majority can change the constitution and "cardinal laws." The president, elected by parliament, is largely ceremonial.

**Who holds power:** Prime Minister Péter Magyar (Tisza), in office since 9 May 2026.

### Netherlands

In 2023 Geert Wilders, the Netherlands' most prominent anti-Islam politician, won an election for the first time. His coalition, the most right-wing in modern Dutch history, lasted 11 months before he pulled out over asylum laws, triggering a snap election in October 2025. This time the winner, by a narrow margin decided only after postal ballots were counted, was the centrist D66 under Rob Jetten.

On 23 February 2026, Jetten was sworn in at 38 as the Netherlands' youngest prime minister and its first openly gay one ([AP via WTOP](https://wtop.com/world/2026/02/new-dutch-coalition-government-led-by-youngest-premier-has-been-sworn-into-office/)). His coalition with the centre-right VVD and Christian Democrats is something new for the Dutch: a minority government, holding just 66 of 150 seats with no formal outside support, so every law needs votes from the opposition ([HSS](https://europe.hss.de/en/news/jetten-i-a-new-minority-government-in-the-netherlands-news13782/)). Wilders's PVV came second but every coalition party ruled him out. Jetten promised to bring the Netherlands "back to the heart of Europe," keep up support for Ukraine and raise defence spending, but his coalition also announced deep cuts to welfare and health care and kept much of the previous government's immigration line. After four elections since 2017, the Dutch are testing whether a deliberately consensual style can outlast the fragmentation that brought Wilders to power.

**How power works:** A constitutional monarchy with a parliamentary system. The prime minister needs the confidence of the 150-seat House of Representatives, elected by nationwide proportional representation with almost no threshold, which produces many parties; the indirectly elected Senate can block laws.

**Who holds power:** Prime Minister Rob Jetten (D66), in office since 23 February 2026.

### Portugal

Portugal spent half a century, since its 1974 Carnation Revolution ended dictatorship, as a country where the far right barely existed. That changed with Chega ("Enough"), founded in 2019 by a former TV football pundit, André Ventura, which became the main opposition party in May 2025, the country's third election in three years. In 2026 Ventura reached for the presidency.

The first round on 18 January was disrupted by deadly storms and floods in central and southern Portugal, which forced voting to be suspended in some areas; Ventura demanded the whole election be postponed. He came second with 23.5 percent behind the Socialist António José Seguro, becoming the first far-right candidate ever to reach a Portuguese runoff ([AFP via BSS](https://www.bssnews.net/international/359098)). On 8 February, Seguro, a 63-year-old former Socialist leader who had been out of politics for a decade, won the runoff with about two-thirds of the vote, as most of the centre and centre-right rallied behind him ([NPR via Michigan Public](https://www.michiganpublic.org/2026-02-09/center-left-socialist-candidate-wins-over-populist-in-portugals-presidential-runoff)). Ventura still took more than 1.5 million votes, his party's best result, and declared that Chega "will soon govern this country." The presidency is mostly ceremonial, but Portuguese presidents can dissolve parliament, a power that has mattered a great deal in a country that keeps going back to the polls.

**How power works:** A semi-presidential republic. The prime minister leads the government with the support of the 230-seat Assembly of the Republic; the directly elected president has limited executive power but can veto laws and dissolve parliament.

**Who holds power:** Prime Minister Luís Montenegro (Democratic Alliance); President António José Seguro, since March 2026.

### Serbia

On 1 November 2024, a newly renovated concrete canopy at the railway station in Novi Sad collapsed, killing 16 people. Serbians saw it as the product of a corrupt state that cut corners on public works, and students launched protests that grew into the largest movement against Aleksandar Vučić in his more than a decade in power. Nearly two years on, they forced him into an early election.

Vučić dissolved parliament on 9 September and called a snap election for 25 October. On 27 September, seven months before his second and final presidential term was due to end, he resigned the presidency so he can become prime minister if his Serbian Progressive Party wins ([Reuters via The Print](https://theprint.in/world/serbias-president-vucic-resigns-ahead-of-october-parliamentary-elections/3055161/)). It is a familiar manoeuvre for leaders facing term limits. His ally Ana Brnabić, the parliament speaker, is acting president, and a presidential election must be held by the end of the year. The main challengers are Students Win, the anti-corruption movement born from the protests, and European Serbia, an alliance of pro-Western parties. Vučić told CNN he would congratulate the opposition the same evening if it won, but one Serbian analyst told Al Jazeera she feared "the crisis could only begin after the October 25 elections" ([Al Jazeera](https://aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/27/serbian-president-aleksandar-vucic-resigns-amid-prolonged-protests)). Serbia balances between the EU, which it wants to join, and Russia and China, which it courts.

**How power works:** A parliamentary republic. The prime minister leads the government with the support of the 250-seat National Assembly, elected by proportional representation; the president is directly elected for up to two five-year terms. Under Vučić the presidency became the real centre of power, which may now shift with him.

**Who holds power:** Aleksandar Vučić (SNS), seeking the premiership; Acting President Ana Brnabić.

### Slovenia

Janez Janša has been part of Slovenian politics since he was jailed by the Yugoslav army in 1988 for exposing its secrets, an arrest that helped spark the country's independence. He went on to serve as prime minister three times and became one of Europe's most combative populists, an admirer of Trump and an ally of Orbán. When Slovenians voted him out in 2022, many assumed he was finished. In 2026, at 67, he came back.

The 22 March election was nearly a tie: Prime Minister Robert Golob's liberal Freedom Movement won 29 seats and Janša's SDS 28 ([Reuters via Internazionale](https://www.internazionale.it/ultime-notizie-reuters/2026/05/19/slovenia-s-jansa-seeks-fourth-term-after-coalition-secures-majority)). Golob failed to form a government. Janša then assembled a coalition with four centre-right parties plus outside support from Resni.ca, an anti-establishment party whose leader, Zoran Stevanović, rose through protests against Janša and signed a notarised pledge never to join a Janša government ([New Eastern Europe](https://neweasterneurope.eu/2026/05/29/the-fourth-government-of-janez-jansa-is-becoming-a-reality-in-slovenia/)). Stevanović became speaker. On 22 May, parliament elected Janša prime minister by 51 votes to 36, making him Slovenia's oldest ever premier; his cabinet took office on 4 June, promising tax cuts and pension reform ([bne IntelliNews](https://www.intellinews.com/slovenian-mps-vote-to-bring-back-janez-jan-a-for-fourth-term-as-pm-444523/)). With Orbán gone from Hungary, Janša is now one of the few leaders of his kind left inside the EU.

**How power works:** A parliamentary republic. The prime minister is elected by the 90-seat National Assembly; the directly elected president, Nataša Pirc Musar, nominates candidates and has limited powers.

**Who holds power:** Prime Minister Janez Janša (SDS), in office since May 2026.

### Spain

On 19 July, Spain beat Argentina 1-0 in extra time at MetLife Stadium to win the World Cup, the country's second. Two months later, Spaniards were back in the streets, this time in anger. The trigger was the televised eviction of an 87-year-old woman named Maricarmen from her Madrid flat, owned by an investment fund; it crystallised years of fury over a housing market in which average rents have nearly doubled in a decade ([AFP via Oman Observer](https://www.omanobserver.om/article/1197300/world/europe/spains-pm-calls-snap-election-after-housing-bill-defeat)).

Protesters pitched a tent city in Madrid's Puerta del Sol. Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez's minority government proposed two emergency decrees, including a ban on evicting vulnerable tenants until 2030 and limits on purchases by investment funds. On 2 October, the conservative PP, the far-right Vox and the Catalan separatists of Junts, whose votes the government relies on, defeated them. The next day the government said 70,000 people marched in Madrid; organisers claimed half a million. On 5 October, Sánchez called a snap election for 29 November, ten months early, asking voters to choose between joining "the far-right wave" and keeping Spain "a bastion of openness" ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/amp/news/2026/10/5/spanish-prime-minister-pedro-sanchez-announces-snap-election)). Polls favour a PP government dependent on Vox. Sánchez is also under fire for corruption cases and for the crisis in Ceuta, where about 70,000 people crossed from Morocco in 48 hours. If he loses, one of Europe's last left-wing governments falls.

**How power works:** A constitutional monarchy with a parliamentary system. The prime minister needs the confidence of the 350-seat Congress of Deputies, elected by provincial proportional representation; regional governments, especially in Catalonia and the Basque Country, hold wide powers. King Felipe VI is head of state.

**Who holds power:** Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez (PSOE), facing an election on 29 November.

### Sweden

For four years, Sweden was governed by a centre-right coalition that depended on the votes of the Sweden Democrats, a party with roots in the neo-Nazi movement that became the country's second largest. It was a historic break in a country long seen as Europe's model of social democracy, and the government took a hard line on immigration and gang crime. In September, Swedes narrowly reversed it, and then found that winning was the easy part.

In the 13 September election, the four left-leaning parties won 176 of 349 seats against 173 for Prime Minister Ulf Kristersson's bloc, and the Sweden Democrats lost ground, putting Sweden at odds with the far-right rise across much of Europe ([L'Orient-Le Jour](https://today.lorientlejour.com/article/1547888/swedens-prime-minister-steps-down-after-losing-election.html)). Kristersson resigned on 17 September, and the speaker asked Social Democrat leader Magdalena Andersson, Sweden's first woman prime minister in 2021-22, to form a government ([Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2026/09/18/swedens-social-democrat-leader-andersson-says-shes-ready-to-form-new-government-after-elec)). Her bloc was fractured: the Centre Party and the Left Party disagree sharply. On 28 September, after the Left Party backed a centre-right candidate for speaker, Andersson said there was "currently no possibility" for her to form a government ([Reuters via Oman Observer](https://www.omanobserver.om/article/1196906/world/europe/sweden-faces-political-deadlock-after-failing-to-form-new-govt)). The speaker can put up to four candidates to a vote; if all fail, Sweden must hold a new election. Kristersson stays on as caretaker.

**How power works:** A constitutional monarchy with a parliamentary system of "negative parliamentarism": a prime minister takes office unless an absolute majority of the 349-seat Riksdag votes against. King Carl XVI Gustaf is ceremonial.

**Who holds power:** Caretaker Prime Minister Ulf Kristersson (Moderates), while government formation continues.

### Ukraine

Russia's full-scale invasion has now lasted more than four and a half years, longer than the Soviet Union's war against Nazi Germany. Russia holds about a fifth of Ukraine. In 2026 the war did not end, but the effort to end it kept being interrupted by other crises.

A US-brokered ceasefire for 9 to 11 May was broken almost at once, with each side accusing the other of thousands of violations and fighting continuing along a 745-mile front ([Reuters via Defense News](https://development.defensenews.com/?p=50084)). Washington's peace push stalled in March when its attention shifted to the war with Iran. Russia's foreign minister declared in August that "there will be no ceasefire," and Moscow kept demanding Ukraine hand over parts of the east its troops have not captured. Meanwhile, both sides escalated long-range strikes: the UN counted 15,280 civilian casualties in the first eight months of 2026, up 55 percent on the year before, the worst since the early days of the war ([Political.org tracker](https://political.org/russia-ukraine-war-tracker/)). In September, the Kremlin said three-way talks with Ukraine and the US could resume in October, and Zelensky said he would meet Putin at the G20 summit in Miami in December ([AFP via Ahram](https://english.ahram.org.eg/News/576575.aspx)). Forecasters put the chance of a ceasefire this year at about 15 percent. For Ukrainians, the reality is another winter of attacks on power plants and homes.

**How power works:** A semi-presidential republic under martial law since 2022, which has suspended elections. The president leads foreign and defence policy; the prime minister and cabinet answer to the 450-seat Verkhovna Rada.

**Who holds power:** President Volodymyr Zelensky, in office since 2019, with Kyrylo Budanov as head of the presidential office.

## Middle East

### Iran

For 37 years, Iran had one supreme leader, Ali Khamenei, who survived wars, sanctions and repeated waves of protest, most recently a nationwide uprising in January 2026. On 28 February, in the opening hours of a joint US-Israeli assault, he was killed along with several senior commanders, reportedly at a meeting about how to crush those protests ([Iran International](https://www.iranintl.com/en/202607243613)). The Assembly of Experts named his son, Mojtaba, as successor, turning the Islamic Republic's highest office into something close to a hereditary one.

Iran hit back at Israel and at US bases and Gulf states across the region, and above all it closed the Strait of Hormuz, through which about a fifth of the world's oil had passed. Oil prices soared, and countries from Singapore to Belgium felt it. After roughly 40 days, a ceasefire in April led to the highest-level US-Iran talks in half a century, in Islamabad. A preliminary deal followed in June, but it lapsed; strikes and Iranian missile fire resumed over the summer. By late August, Iran's deputy foreign minister was still saying flatly that "the Strait of Hormuz is closed," with ships allowed through only in coordination with Iran, and Tehran and Oman were working on a joint shipping lane and even sharing toll revenue ([Al Jazeera via UDLAP](https://observatorioglobal.udlap.mx/?p=11284)). Mojtaba Khamenei, reportedly injured in the first strikes, has not appeared in public since February, communicating through written messages that vow revenge. At home, the regime has tightened its grip, including a harder line on compulsory hijab. Trump said in August he was "not in a hurry" to resume talks.

**How power works:** A theocratic republic. The supreme leader, chosen for life by the clerical Assembly of Experts, controls the armed forces, the Revolutionary Guards, the judiciary and state media. An elected president and parliament run day-to-day government, but candidates are vetted by the clerical Guardian Council.

**Who holds power:** Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei, since March 2026; President Masoud Pezeshkian.

### Iraq

Iraq has spent two decades since the 2003 US invasion trying to be friends with both Washington and Tehran, its two most powerful patrons and now enemies at war. In 2026 that balancing act came close to breaking.

After elections in November 2025, government formation stalled for months, partly because the dominant bloc of Shia parties nominated Nouri al-Maliki, a two-time former prime minister close to Iran, and Trump warned in January that the US would stop helping Iraq if Maliki returned ([AFP via BSS](https://www.bssnews.net/international/376845)). Then the Iran war began, and Iraq became a battlefield again: Iran-aligned militias fired on US bases and the US embassy, and on Jordan, while American strikes hit them back. On 11 April, parliament finally elected Nizar Amedi, a 58-year-old Kurdish former environment minister, as president with 227 votes. "This ongoing conflict is not Iraq's war," he said, urging parties to keep the country out of it ([Peregraf](https://peregraf.com/en/news/11473)). Later that month, the US put bounties on two militia leaders, and the Shia bloc named businessman Ali al-Zaidi as prime minister-designate instead of Maliki ([ISHM](https://enablingpeace.org/2026/04/)). Behind the politics, Iraq faces a slower crisis Amedi knows well from his last job: its rivers are drying up as upstream dams and climate change cut the flow of the Tigris and Euphrates.

**How power works:** A federal parliamentary republic with an informal sectarian power-sharing rule: the prime minister, who holds real power, is a Shia Arab; the speaker a Sunni Arab; and the largely ceremonial president a Kurd. The 329-seat Council of Representatives elects the president, who then nominates a prime minister. The Kurdistan Region has its own government.

**Who holds power:** President Nizar Amedi (PUK); prime ministerial nominee Ali al-Zaidi, whose confirmation I could not verify.

### Israel

On 7 October 2023, Hamas killed about 1,200 people in southern Israel, the deadliest day for Jews since the Holocaust, on the watch of Benjamin Netanyahu, who had built his career as "Mr Security." What followed was a war in Gaza that killed tens of thousands of Palestinians and expanded to Lebanon, Syria, Yemen and in 2026 a direct war with Iran. Polls since 2023 have mostly predicted Netanyahu would lose the next election. On 27 October, Israelis finally get to vote.

It will be the first Israeli election held after a full four-year term since 1988. The opposition has consolidated: in April, former prime ministers Naftali Bennett, a right-wing former commando turned tech millionaire, and the centrist Yair Lapid merged their parties into a joint list called Together, led by Bennett ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/4/26/former-israeli-pms-bennett-lapid-unite-to-challenge-netanyahu-in-elections)). Former army chief Gadi Eisenkot leads another new party, Yashar. The pair united once before, in 2021, to end Netanyahu's 12 years in power, only for their coalition to collapse in 18 months. The race is tight. A Kan 11 poll in late September had Netanyahu ahead of Bennett 42 to 33 as preferred prime minister, while seat projections show Netanyahu's right-wing and religious bloc short of the 61 seats needed for a majority ([Wikipedia polling table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leadership_opinion_polling_for_the_2026_Israeli_legislative_election)). Netanyahu, still on trial for corruption and seeking a pardon, has vowed to "do everything to win." The killing of Iran's supreme leader has given him a new security story to tell.

**How power works:** A parliamentary democracy with no written constitution. The prime minister needs a 61-seat majority in the 120-seat Knesset, elected by nationwide proportional representation with a 3.25 percent threshold, so every government is a coalition; the president is ceremonial but chooses who gets the first chance to form one.

**Who holds power:** Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu (Likud), facing an election on 27 October.

## Territories

### Kosovo

In 1998-99, ethnic Albanian guerrillas of the Kosovo Liberation Army fought Serbian forces until NATO's bombing campaign forced Belgrade out. Kosovo declared independence in 2008; about 100 countries recognise it, but Serbia, Russia and China do not, and five EU states still refuse. Many Kosovars see the KLA's fighters as liberators. In 2026, an international court in The Hague called some of them war criminals, and Kosovo's politics nearly broke over it.

On 16 September, the Kosovo Specialist Chambers, a court set up under Western pressure to try KLA crimes, convicted former president Hashim Thaçi and three other wartime leaders, sentencing them to a combined 81 years ([Reuters via The Print](https://theprint.in/world/kosovo-parliament-elects-new-president-avoiding-snap-election/3063879/)). More than two weeks of protests followed, some violent, as people feared the verdict would tarnish the independence struggle ([AP via ClickOnDetroit](https://www.clickondetroit.com/news/world/2026/10/07/kosovo-lawmakers-elect-a-new-president-in-a-late-night-vote-to-avert-snap-election/)). At the same time, parliament faced a deadline: Kosovo's president needs a two-thirds vote, and failure in June had already forced one snap election. Just after midnight on 7 October, three minutes past the constitutional deadline, lawmakers elected economist Justina Shiroka Pula, head of the Academy of Sciences and Arts, with 81 votes, one more than required ([European Western Balkans](https://europeanwesternbalkans.com/2026/10/07/justina-shiroka-pula-elected-president-of-kosovo-by-parliament/)). The price of the deal between Prime Minister Albin Kurti's Vetëvendosje and the opposition PDK, Thaçi's old party, was a law limiting the Hague court's powers, which will anger the Western backers Kosovo depends on.

**How power works:** A parliamentary republic. The prime minister leads the government with the support of the 120-seat Assembly, which includes 20 seats reserved for minorities; the president, elected by a two-thirds vote, is largely ceremonial but commands the armed forces and must sign laws.

**Who holds power:** Prime Minister Albin Kurti (Vetëvendosje); President Justina Shiroka Pula, since 7 October 2026.
