Part 5 adds 21 states, current to 12 October 2026, and is the first written in a new way: each entry starts with why its story matters, the history that gives this year's numbers their weight, before the year's events. Two threads tie this batch together. One is the Iran war's reach far beyond the Gulf, into Singapore's refineries, Belgium's wage fights, Norway's budget and Bahrain's streets. The other is old men and old wounds: Ouattara at 84, Ortega at 80, Rabuka the former coup leader, and countries like Cyprus, Greece and Bougainville still living with what happened decades ago.

## Africa

### Côte d'Ivoire

Fifteen years ago, more than 3,000 Ivorians died because one man, Laurent Gbagbo, would not accept that he had lost an election to Alassane Ouattara. Ouattara came to power as the democrat in that story. In 2026 he began a fourth term at 84, having won it after Gbagbo and banker Tidjane Thiam, the most credible younger challenger, were both struck off the ballot.

The October 2025 vote gave Ouattara nearly 90 percent, but barely half the electorate turned out after the excluded opposition called a boycott and denounced a "civilian coup" ([BBC via Africa.com](https://africa.com/?p=251768)). In December the ruling RHDP took 197 of 255 parliamentary seats ([International IDEA](https://www.idea.int/democracytracker/node/?page=5)). Ouattara got around the two-term limit the same way many leaders in the region have, with a 2016 constitution that, he argues, reset the count to zero. The country he runs is one of Africa's real economic successes: the world's top cocoa producer, with new offshore oil and a mining boom. Political tension is now a question of age rather than ballots. Ouattara has no obvious heir, and the man he once said should hand over to a new generation is still in charge. The 2026 headlines that reached ordinary Ivorians were smaller: 20 people killed by flooding in Abidjan, and a World Cup exit to Norway.

**How power works:** A presidential republic with a strong executive. The president is directly elected for five years and appoints the prime minister, vice president and government; parliament has a National Assembly and an appointed-heavy Senate.

**Who holds power:** President Alassane Ouattara (RHDP), in office since 2011.

### Ghana

Ghana's gold is literally poisoning its water. Illegal miners, known as galamsey, have turned rivers like the Pra and Ankobra the colour of milky tea, killing fish, ruining cocoa farms and forcing water utilities to shut treatment plants. President John Mahama won back the presidency in late 2024 promising to stop it. Two years in, it remains the weakest part of his record, and critics say the reason is simple: gold exports are keeping the economy afloat after Ghana's 2022 default, so no government wants to choke them off ([KAS](https://www.kas.de/de/laenderberichte/detail/-/content/ein-jahr-mahama-und-ndc-regierung-in-ghana), [Citi Newsroom](https://citinewsroom.com/2025/09/galamsey-economy-sustained-by-gold-mahama-cant-declare-state-of-emergency-akwasi-konadu/)).

Everything else looked like recovery. In his 2026 State of the Nation address, Mahama said the economy was "in take-off mode" and signed the law creating his 24-Hour Economy Authority on 19 February, his plan to create jobs by keeping factories, ports and services running around the clock for a country where young people struggle most to find work ([GBC](https://www.gbcghanaonline.com/features/ghana-economic-reset-faces-implementation-test-as-mahama-enters-critical-second-year/2026/)). Abroad, Ghana pushed a UN resolution declaring the transatlantic slave trade a crime against humanity, a claim with real weight from a country whose coastal forts held enslaved people before the crossing. Ghana remains West Africa's steadiest democracy, with power changing hands peacefully at the ballot box, which makes the galamsey failure more pointed: it is a problem voters chose Mahama to fix.

**How power works:** A presidential republic. The president is directly elected for four years with a two-term limit and heads the government; the 276-seat Parliament is elected by first-past-the-post. Two parties, Mahama's NDC and the NPP, have alternated in power since 1992.

**Who holds power:** President John Dramani Mahama (NDC), in office since January 2025.

### Libya

Fifteen years after Muammar Gaddafi was killed in a drainage pipe, Libya still has two governments, two parliaments and, in practice, two armies, sitting on Africa's largest oil reserves. The internationally recognised government in Tripoli under Abdul Hamid Dbeibah controls the west. The east and south answer to the 82-year-old field marshal Khalifa Haftar and a rival cabinet under Osama Hamad. The 2021 elections that were meant to end this were cancelled days before polling, and both camps have found the division comfortable.

2026 showed the cost. On 8 May, fighting with heavy weapons between Tripoli-aligned security forces and local militias forced Libya's largest refinery, at Zawiya, to shut, with shells landing in civilian neighbourhoods ([Security Council Report](https://www.securitycouncilreport.org/monthly-forecast/2026-06/libya-68.php?)). Drone strikes later hit refinery facilities, and parts of the country suffered fuel and power shortages despite sitting on all that oil. Then, on 30 August, after months of UN-led talks, the rival sides signed an agreement in Tripoli to hold national elections within two years, which the Security Council called an important step ([Africanews](https://www.africanews.com/2026/09/09/un-hails-libyan-agreement-to-move-toward-elections/)). Libyans have heard promises like this before. The question is whether the men who benefit from the split will let a vote remove them.

**How power works:** No single authority. The UN-recognised Government of National Unity and a Presidential Council govern from Tripoli; the eastern House of Representatives backs a rival Government of National Stability and Haftar's Libyan National Army. Armed groups hold real power on the ground in both halves.

**Who holds power:** Prime Minister Abdul Hamid Dbeibah in Tripoli; Field Marshal Khalifa Haftar in the east.

### Niger

On the night of 29 January, gunmen on motorbikes, backed by drones, attacked the military base beside Niamey's international airport. Stored there was roughly 1,000 tonnes of uranium yellowcake, seized from the French company Orano after Niger's military took its mine. The attackers were driven off, with about 20 killed, and the uranium was unharmed ([MiningMX/Bloomberg](https://miningmx.com/news/energy/2026-01-30-niger-airport-attack-perilously-close-to-uranium-stockpile/), [Monitor/Reuters](https://www.monitor.co.ug/uganda/news/niger-accuses-france-and-neighbours-of-airport-siege-5343518)). That night captured Niger's 2026: a junta that took power promising security, sitting on a strategic mineral it took from France, unable to protect its own capital.

General Abdourahamane Tiani, who ousted elected president Mohamed Bazoum in 2023, blamed the presidents of France, Benin and Côte d'Ivoire by name, without evidence, and thanked Russian troops stationed at the base. The airport was hit again in June, an attack claimed by the Islamic State ([Modern Ghana](https://www.modernghana.com/news/1504819/when-the-finger-points-to-paris-nigers-niamey.html)). In March, attacks killed at least 44 civilians in the southwest. Then on 29 August came a different threat: gunfire and explosions around the airport and the presidency in what sources described as an attack by mutinous soldiers ([Reuters via TimesLive](https://www.timeslive.co.za/news/africa/2026-08-29-mutinous-soldiers-attack-airport-presidency-in-niger-capital-sources-say/)). Days earlier, the junta had reassigned Orano's former uranium permits to state-backed firms. Niger once supplied about 15 percent of Orano's uranium, fuel for French reactors; it is now trying to sell it on its own terms, while the state struggles to hold Niamey.

**How power works:** Military rule. Tiani heads the National Council for the Safeguard of the Homeland, governing by decree under a 2025 transition charter that gives him a five-year term. Parties are dissolved. Niger left ECOWAS and formed the Alliance of Sahel States with Mali and Burkina Faso.

**Who holds power:** General Abdourahamane Tiani.

### Senegal

In March 2024, Senegal did something rare: it put two men straight from prison into power. Ousmane Sonko was the opposition's star but was barred from running, so his protégé Bassirou Diomaye Faye ran instead on the slogan "Diomaye is Sonko," won, and made Sonko prime minister. Two years later the slogan is dead. On 22 May, Faye fired Sonko and dissolved the government ([Reuters via Citizen Digital](https://citizen.digital/article/senegal-president-sacks-prime-minister-sonko-dissolves-govt-n383299)).

The break was about money. The new government discovered that its predecessor had hidden around $13 billion in debt, pushing the real figure to about 132 percent of GDP. The IMF froze a $1.8 billion programme. Faye wanted to negotiate with the Fund, possibly including a debt restructuring; Sonko called restructuring a "disgrace" and argued Senegal could raise taxes at home instead ([Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/a-new-schism-in-senegal-marks-uncharted-territory-for-africas-democratic-beacon/)). Behind the policy fight was a succession one: whether Faye would run again in 2029 or step aside for Sonko. Sonko did not disappear. By June he was speaker of the National Assembly, which his Pastef party dominates, and was installed as party leader at a rally in Dakar, and he later softened his line on IMF talks ([bne IntelliNews](https://www.intellinews.com/senegal-s-powerful-ex-pm-ousmane-sonko-opens-door-to-imf-debt-talks-as-fiscal-crisis-deepens-449272/)). Senegal is now a president governing against the man who made him, with the legislature in his rival's hands, while it tries to dig out of a debt hole that will shape public spending for a decade.

**How power works:** A presidential republic. The president is directly elected for five years with a two-term limit and appoints the prime minister; the 165-seat National Assembly can censure the government. Senegal has never had a coup.

**Who holds power:** President Bassirou Diomaye Faye; Ousmane Sonko controls the ruling party and the Assembly.

### South Sudan

The world's youngest country has never held a national election. South Sudan won independence in 2011 amid huge hope, and within two and a half years its president, Salva Kiir, a Dinka, and his deputy, Riek Machar, a Nuer, had led their communities into a civil war that killed some 400,000 people. A 2018 peace deal put them back in government together. In 2026, Machar spent the year on trial for treason, murder and terrorism, under house arrest, while the government insisted the first elections would finally happen in December ([AP via Yahoo](https://www.yahoo.com/news/articles/treason-trial-south-sudans-suspended-201125236.html)).

The charges stem from a March 2025 clash in Upper Nile that the government blamed on Machar's allies. His trial, with seven co-defendants, dragged past its 68th session, and the African Union called for his release to allow a ceasefire and dialogue ([Radio Tamazuj](https://www.radiotamazuj.org/en/news/article/justice-minister-acknowledges-delays-in-machar-case)). UN experts warned that the peace deal was eroding, that both sides' forces faced off across much of the country, and that an under-supplied army was relying on "relatively indiscriminate" air strikes, with Ugandan troops backing Kiir. The information minister vowed elections in December with "no further extension" ([Arab News](https://www.arabnews.pk/node/2640724)). Analysts say the trial's real purpose is to keep Machar out of any vote. Meanwhile, war in neighbouring Sudan has cut oil exports and sent refugees south, in a country where most people already depend on aid.

**How power works:** A transitional government under the 2018 peace agreement, sharing posts among Kiir's SPLM, Machar's SPLM-IO and other parties, with a president, five vice presidents and an expanded appointed parliament. In practice Kiir rules by decree, frequently firing officials without consultation.

**Who holds power:** President Salva Kiir, in office since independence in 2011.

## Americas

### Jamaica

On 28 October 2025, Hurricane Melissa came ashore in Westmoreland as a Category 5 storm, crossed western Jamaica and left over Trelawny. It was, in the prime minister's words, the third most powerful hurricane ever recorded anywhere and the most destructive ever to hit the island ([Office of the Prime Minister](https://opm.gov.jm/2026-new-years-message-from-dr-the-most-honourable-andrew-holness/)). Jamaica spent 2026 rebuilding the west, a year after Hurricane Beryl had already battered it.

The damage was intimate and specific. Black River, a historic market town in St Elizabeth, was among the hardest hit ([Jamaica Observer](https://www.jamaicaobserver.com/2026/01/02/holness-reaffirms-commitment-black-river-recovery/)). Schools, police stations and clinics in the western parishes were wrecked, and the Westmoreland to St Elizabeth corridor will be rebuilt on a new alignment. Tourism, the country's lifeline, lost hotels for months; Sandals kept staff at shuttered resorts on the payroll. By March, Prime Minister Andrew Holness declared the country had moved from relief to reconstruction, with power and water largely restored, farms recovering and inflation low enough for the central bank to cut rates ([JIS](https://jis.gov.jm/jamaica-officially-enters-reconstruction-phase-post-melissa/)). The government says it has committed more than J$67 billion. The frustration that remained was slow money: in June, Holness publicly called on private insurers to pay claims faster because delays were stalling families' rebuilding ([Gleaner](https://jamaica-gleaner.com/index%2Ephp/article/news/20260605/holness-wants-insurers-fast-track-hurricane-melissa-claims)). Planners estimated the economy might not return to its pre-Melissa output until late 2028. The deeper lesson Jamaica is absorbing is that two monster storms in two years may be the new normal for the Caribbean.

**How power works:** A parliamentary democracy and Commonwealth realm, with the British monarch represented by a governor-general. The prime minister leads the majority in the 63-seat elected House of Representatives; two parties, Holness's JLP and the PNP, alternate in power.

**Who holds power:** Prime Minister Andrew Holness (JLP), re-elected for a third term in September 2025.

### Nicaragua

Daniel Ortega first came to power as the revolutionary who overthrew the Somoza family dictatorship in 1979. In 2026, at 80, he presided over something that looks a lot like what he fought: a family dynasty. Since a 2025 constitutional rewrite, his wife Rosario Murillo has been officially "co-president," decrees carry both their signatures, and the presidential term is six years ([Infobae](https://www.infobae.com/nicaragua/2026/07/24/ortega-80-anos-y-un-19-de-julio-bajo-la-lupa-marcha-lenta-frase-aqui-no-volvera-a-haber-elecciones-y-la-sombra-de-la-sucesion/)).

At the 19 July anniversary of the revolution, observers noted how slowly and stiffly Ortega walked, feeding long rumours about his health and the question of what happens when he dies, with Murillo and their children placed throughout the state. In the same week he said there would be no more elections in Nicaragua, so the opposition could never "capture" power ([El Financiero](https://www.elfinanciero.com.mx/mundo/2026/07/21/quien-es-rosario-murillo-poeta-que-ejerce-como-copresidenta-de-nicaragua/)). Three days earlier, Nicaragua broke off relations with Italy; one exiled critic claimed it followed failed talks to secure European guarantees for members of the family. Washington, after seizing Venezuela's Maduro in January, called Murillo's co-presidency invented and illegitimate, which reportedly unsettled Managua. The repression is old by now: thousands of NGOs closed, universities and churches seized, critics stripped of citizenship and expelled since the 2018 protests in which some 300 people died. What is new is the frankness. Nicaragua no longer pretends to be heading anywhere but a succession.

**How power works:** Formally a presidential republic, in practice a one-family dictatorship. The co-presidents control the Sandinista party, the National Assembly, the courts, the electoral council, the police and the army, and since 2025 the constitution subordinates all branches to the presidency.

**Who holds power:** Co-presidents Daniel Ortega and Rosario Murillo.

### Panama

In January 2025, Donald Trump said in his inaugural address that China was operating the Panama Canal and that the United States would take it back. Panama, which spent most of the 20th century fighting to recover the canal from the US, insisted the waterway was its own. In 2026 it removed the thing Trump had pointed at, while trying to make it look like Panama's choice.

The target was two ports at either end of the canal, Balboa on the Pacific and Cristóbal on the Atlantic, run since 1997 by a subsidiary of Hong Kong's CK Hutchison, a conglomerate built by Li Ka-shing. They handled about 38 percent of Panama's container traffic. On 29 January, Panama's Supreme Court declared the concession unconstitutional for granting exclusive privileges and tax breaks; China promised to protect its companies, and Hutchison filed for international arbitration ([AFP via CRHoy](https://crhoy.com/mundo/panama-anula-concesion-a-firma-hongkonesa-en-el-canal-y-china-promete-proteger-a-sus-empresas/)). On 23 February, President José Raúl Mulino ordered the state to temporarily take over the ports, with Denmark's Maersk running them in the transition ([Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/international-trade/panama-occupies-ports-after-court-scraps-ck-hutchison-deal-1)). Mulino said Panama "will not allow itself to be threatened by any country on earth" and that the ports would never again go to a single operator ([Reuters via Yahoo](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/panamas-president-says-port-contracts-164152865.html)). The ruling also left Hutchison's planned sale of its global ports business, which had a US-backed buyer, in limbo. A small country carrying about 5 percent of world shipping trade had become a front line in the US-China rivalry.

**How power works:** A presidential republic. The president is directly elected for a single five-year term by simple plurality and heads the government; the 71-seat National Assembly is unicameral. The canal itself is run by an autonomous authority.

**Who holds power:** President José Raúl Mulino (Realizando Metas), in office since 2024.

## Asia

### Mongolia

Mongolia is a democracy wedged between Russia and China, two of the world's most powerful authoritarian states, and it has stayed one since its peaceful revolution in 1990. That is a real achievement. But in 2026 the country showed how fragile a democracy can be when one party wins everything and then fights itself.

On 27 March, Prime Minister Gombojav Zandanshatar resigned after nine months in office, warning that "the world is entering a critical geopolitical period" ([AFP via Citizen Digital](https://citizen.digital/article/mongolian-pm-resigns-after-months-of-upheaval-n379822)). He had already survived one attempt by parliament to remove him, blocked by the Constitutional Court in October. The pressure came from corruption allegations against his justice minister, a boycott of parliament by the opposition Democratic Party, and above all a feud inside the ruling Mongolian People's Party between factions loyal to President Ukhnaa Khurelsukh and to former prime minister Oyun-Erdene, who had fallen in 2025. Parliament confirmed party chairman and speaker Uchral Nyam-Osor as Mongolia's third prime minister in about a year, seen as a compromise between the factions ([AP via WTOP](https://wtop.com/world/2026/03/mongolia-names-new-prime-minister-in-bid-to-end-legislative-deadlock/)). Why it matters to ordinary Mongolians: the country lives on coal and copper sold to China and imports all its fuel, so the Iran war's oil shock hit hard, and every change of government means shifting mining rules that scare off the investment that pays for pensions and wages.

**How power works:** A semi-presidential democracy. The president is directly elected for a single six-year term and has veto and some security powers; the 126-seat State Great Khural elects the prime minister, who runs the government.

**Who holds power:** Prime Minister Uchral Nyam-Osor (MPP); President Ukhnaa Khurelsukh.

### Singapore

Singapore has no oil, no gas and barely any land, yet it is one of the world's great refining and shipping hubs. That makes it a sensitive gauge of a war thousands of miles away. When Iran choked shipping through the Strait of Hormuz in 2026, Singapore felt it fast.

On 2 April, Prime Minister Lawrence Wong gave a video address telling Singaporeans that oil prices had risen more than 60 percent since the war began, that Asia was "particularly exposed" because so much Gulf energy flows to it, and that no country, "least of all a small island-nation like ours," could be insulated ([Prime Minister's Office](https://www.pmo.gov.sg/newsroom/pm-lawrence-wong-on-the-situation-in-the-middle-east-apr-2026/)). Refineries cut output and sourced crude elsewhere; Australia now supplies over a third of Singapore's LNG; a ministerial crisis committee coordinated the home front, and the government brought forward help for households and businesses. The surprise was resilience. Singapore's economy grew 6 percent in the first quarter, driven by demand for AI chips, though Wong warned in June that slower growth and higher inflation were still coming ([AFP via Malay Mail](https://www.malaymail.com/amp/news/singapore/2026/06/09/despite-strong-q1-singapore-pm-says-h2-economic-outlook-uncertain-as-middle-east-war-drags-on/223105)). A year after he won his first election warning of "storm clouds," the storm arrived, and his People's Action Party, in power since 1959, is being judged on how it weathers it.

**How power works:** A parliamentary republic in the Westminster model. The prime minister leads the majority in Parliament, elected largely through multi-member constituencies that favour the PAP; the elected president has custodial powers over reserves and key appointments.

**Who holds power:** Prime Minister Lawrence Wong (PAP), in office since 2024.

### Uzbekistan

The Aral Sea was once the world's fourth-largest lake. Then Soviet planners diverted the rivers that fed it, chiefly the Amu Darya, to grow cotton in Uzbekistan's desert, and within a few decades most of it was gone, leaving rusting fishing boats on salt flats and dust storms full of pesticide residue. Uzbekistan's 2026 was a reminder that the region's water fights are not over. They are getting worse.

The new threat comes from upstream. Taliban-ruled Afghanistan is building the Qosh Tepa canal, about 285 kilometres long, to turn northern desert into farmland using Amu Darya water. Construction of its second phase was under way in 2026, with operation provisionally set for 2028, and estimates suggest it could cut the river's flow by 15 to 20 percent, water that Uzbek and Turkmen farms depend on ([Times of Central Asia](https://timesca.com/afghan-canal-will-divert-water-from-uzbekistan/)). Afghanistan has no water-sharing agreement with its neighbours. President Shavkat Mirziyoyev, who once warned the canal could "radically change" Central Asia's water balance, has chosen engagement over confrontation: in April he proposed a regional decade of water action, and on 9 October he called for regular, structured water talks with the Taliban ([Afghanistan International](https://www.afintl.com/en/202610096434)). Uzbekistan also won a UN resolution naming the Aral Sea region a zone of environmental innovation. For a country of 37 million whose farms and cities live on two rivers, this is an existential question that does not make headlines.

**How power works:** A presidential republic with a dominant executive. A 2023 constitution extended presidential terms to seven years and reset Mirziyoyev's term count, allowing him to stay until 2037. Parliament has two chambers; all parties support the president and there is no real opposition.

**Who holds power:** President Shavkat Mirziyoyev, in office since 2016.

## Europe

### Austria

In September 2024, Herbert Kickl's Freedom Party won an Austrian national election for the first time since its founding by former Nazis in the 1950s. Every other party had promised not to govern with him. Austria then spent five months proving how hard keeping that promise would be: centrist coalition talks collapsed, the conservatives briefly negotiated with Kickl, and those talks collapsed too. In March 2025, Christian Stocker, a little-known conservative who became chancellor almost by accident, formed a three-party coalition of the centre-right ÖVP, the Social Democrats and the liberal NEOS whose main shared purpose was keeping Kickl out ([CIDOB](https://www.cidob.org/lider-politico/christian-stocker)).

2026 showed the cost of that bargain. To close a large budget deficit in a recession-hit economy tied to Germany's, the coalition passed an unpopular austerity package, and voters blamed it. By summer, polls had the FPÖ at around 34 percent and Stocker's ÖVP at 18 to 19; asked whom they would pick directly as chancellor, only 8 percent chose Stocker and 29 percent chose Kickl ([Börse Express](https://www.boerse-express.com/news/articles/kanzlerfrage-nur-8-fuer-stocker-kickl-erreicht-29-930904)). Stocker went on a summer tour of the country trying to reconnect, while a business-chamber scandal in Vienna gave Kickl fresh material. Commentators argued Stocker's best hope was to make himself the anti-Trump candidate, since Kickl openly admires Trump and Viktor Orbán ([Vol.at](https://www.vol.at/should-kickl-fear-stocker/9957269)). That bet looked better after Orbán himself lost power in Hungary in April. The question hanging over Austria is whether a coalition built to block one man can govern well enough to stop him winning outright next time.

**How power works:** A federal parliamentary republic. The chancellor leads the government with the support of the 183-seat National Council, elected by proportional representation; the directly elected federal president, Alexander Van der Bellen, appoints the chancellor and can refuse to.

**Who holds power:** Chancellor Christian Stocker (ÖVP), in a coalition with the SPÖ and NEOS.

### Belgium

Belgium has a famous habit of going without a government, once for 541 days. In 2025 it finally got one led, unusually, by a Flemish nationalist, Bart De Wever, whose party wants eventually to dissolve the Belgian state. His promise was to govern "like the IMF": cut a budget deficit that breaks EU rules, rework pensions and limit how long people can draw unemployment benefits ([Brussels Times](https://brusselstimes.com/belgium/1575354/titanic-is-sinking-100-days-of-belgiums-new-federal-government/)).

Working Belgium answered by walking out, again and again. On 12 March 2026, police counted about 80,000 people marching in Brussels in a general strike, with unions claiming over 100,000 ([Euronews](https://euronews.com/video/2026/03/13/tens-of-thousands-protest-austerity-reforms-in-brussels)). Another nationwide strike on 12 May grounded flights and stopped trains, with 40,000 to 70,000 marching, many carrying pictures of De Wever labelled "pension thief" ([Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/my-europe/2026/05/12/general-strike-in-belgium-against-government-reforms-draws-tens-of-thousands-to-the-capita)). The anger centres on three things: a pension "malus" that cuts payouts for people who retire early, limits on automatic wage indexation, the system that raises pay with prices and matters more than ever with energy costs pushed up by the Iran war, and cuts to benefits. The unions want a tax on large fortunes instead. The government says it needs roughly €10 billion in savings and that the reforms are what every other European country has already done. It is a classic European fight over who pays for an ageing society, playing out in the capital of the EU.

**How power works:** A federal constitutional monarchy. King Philippe is head of state; the prime minister leads a federal coalition that must balance Dutch- and French-speaking parties, since governments need majorities on both sides of the language border. Regions and language communities hold large powers of their own.

**Who holds power:** Prime Minister Bart De Wever (N-VA), leading the five-party "Arizona" coalition.

### Cyprus

Cyprus has been divided since 1974, when Turkey invaded after a Greek-backed coup, and Turkish troops still hold the north. That wound shapes everything. In 2026 the island's parliamentary election showed it being reopened by a party that wants no compromise.

On 24 May, the conservative Democratic Rally came first with 17 seats and the communist AKEL second, each keeping exactly what they won in 2021. The story was underneath. ELAM, a far-right party founded as an offshoot of Greece's neo-Nazi Golden Dawn, doubled its seats to eight with nearly 11 percent, campaigning on migration, national identity and a harder line toward Turkey ([National Herald](https://www.thenationalherald.com/cyprus-election-clear-win-for-democratic-rally-akel-second-fidias-party-enters-parliament/)). Two newcomers also entered: the anti-corruption ALMA, and Direct Democracy, founded months earlier by Fidias Panayiotou, a YouTuber who was elected to the European Parliament in 2024 and promises to let citizens direct their MPs through an app. The two old parties took 51 percent between them, down from nearly 69 percent in 2001, and the centrist parties backing President Nikos Christodoulides lost most of their seats ([Who Governs](https://whogoverns.eu/cyprus-2026-stability-at-the-top-revolt-at-the-bottom/)). Because Cyprus is presidential, the vote did not change the government, but it is a clear warning before the 2028 presidential race. The 24 seats reserved for Turkish Cypriots have stood empty since the 1960s breakdown, a reminder of the island's other half.

**How power works:** A presidential republic. The president, directly elected for five years, is head of state and government and appoints ministers; the House of Representatives legislates but cannot bring down the government. The Republic's authority does not extend to the Turkish-controlled north.

**Who holds power:** President Nikos Christodoulides, in office since 2023.

### Greece

On 28 February 2023, a passenger train and a freight train collided head-on at Tempi in central Greece, killing 57 people, many of them students heading home from carnival. Investigations found that basic safety systems on the line had never been finished despite years of EU funding. Three years later, Tempi is still the centre of Greek politics, and in 2026 a second scandal joined it.

That one is about farm subsidies. European prosecutors found that fraudsters, many in Crete, had claimed EU money for land they did not own and animals that did not exist, with help from officials at the payments agency OPEKEPE. In July the European Public Prosecutor indicted four lawmakers from Prime Minister Kyriakos Mitsotakis's New Democracy party, including its former political secretary ([AFP via Neos Kosmos](https://neoskosmos.com/en/2026/07/25/news/greece/under-pressure-greek-pm-rules-out-early-elections/)). Mitsotakis, whose family has dominated Cretan politics for a century, abolished the agency, moved its work to the tax authority and said the fraud predated him, while farmers blocked highways and airports over late payments. Polls in the winter put the government's satisfaction rating at about 27 percent; the most admired public figure was Maria Karystianou, the mother of a Tempi victim who has led the fight for accountability and is weighing her own party ([To Vima](https://www.tovima.com/politics/poll-nd-leads-karystianou-popularity-surges/)). New Democracy still leads every poll because the opposition is fractured, and Mitsotakis has ruled out autumn elections. The Greek story is not economic collapse anymore. It is a country that recovered from its debt crisis and now asks whether its state can be trusted.

**How power works:** A parliamentary republic. The prime minister leads the majority in the 300-seat Hellenic Parliament, elected by a system that gives the winning party bonus seats; the president, elected by parliament, is largely ceremonial.

**Who holds power:** Prime Minister Kyriakos Mitsotakis (New Democracy), in office since 2019.

### Norway

Norway struck oil in the North Sea in 1969 and made a choice most petrostates never make: it saved the money. The result is the Government Pension Fund Global, worth around 21 trillion kroner, about $2 trillion, at the end of 2025, which owns on average 1.5 percent of every listed company on Earth ([The Local](https://www.thelocal.no/20260129/today-in-norway-a-roundup-of-the-latest-news-on-thursday-221)). In 2026, when war in the Gulf sent oil and gas prices soaring and Europe scrambled for energy, Norway, Europe's biggest gas supplier, was one of the few countries the crisis made richer. The interesting part is that its politics are now arguments about the ethics of that wealth.

Prime Minister Jonas Gahr Støre's Labour Party won re-election in 2025, but as a minority it needs left-wing partners, and they nearly sank its first budget. The Greens, who want oil wound down by 2040, walked out over new exploration; the Socialist Left walked out demanding the fund sell all Israeli holdings, while the government would only exclude firms tied to the occupation of Palestinian territory ([Reuters via Arctic Today](https://www.arctictoday.com/norway-government-budget-in-peril-over-oil-wealth-funds-israel-investments)). A compromise passed, with the budget drawing a record 579 billion kroner from the fund, still within the rule that spending stay near the fund's expected 3 percent return ([Oilprice](https://oilprice.com/Latest-Energy-News/World-News/Norway-to-Boost-Spending-from-Its-2-Trillion-Oil-Fund-in-2026-Budget.amp.html)). Norwegians also had their own good news: a record 41 medals at the Milan-Cortina Winter Olympics, and a World Cup run that included knocking out Côte d'Ivoire.

**How power works:** A constitutional monarchy with a parliamentary system. King Harald V is head of state; the prime minister needs the confidence of the 169-seat Storting, elected for fixed four-year terms with no early dissolution.

**Who holds power:** Prime Minister Jonas Gahr Støre (Labour), leading a minority government.

## Oceania

### Fiji

In 1987, a young lieutenant colonel named Sitiveni Rabuka walked into Fiji's parliament with masked soldiers and overthrew the elected government, the first of two coups he led that year in the name of indigenous Fijian dominance. Thirty-five years later he became prime minister again, this time by winning an election, and has spent his second career trying to cast himself as a reconciler, including backing a truth and reconciliation commission into the coups he himself began. In 2026, as Fiji prepared for an election due by early 2027, the old question came back: can the country move past its military men?

The year was rough for his government. Two deputy prime ministers resigned after being charged by the anti-corruption commission FICAC, one of them Biman Prasad, leader of a coalition partner, prompting claims Rabuka was using the agency against rivals, which he denied ([Mai TV](https://maitvfiji.com/fiji-pm-denies-people-alliance-strategy-to-consolidate-power-ahead-of-2026-elections/)). A High Court ruling in favour of the anti-corruption chief he had dismissed embarrassed him further. Opposition figure Savenaca Narube, a former central bank governor, told voters not to "choose military people" to lead again; Rabuka, now 77, replied that Narube did not understand democracy ([Fiji Sun](https://fijisun.com.fj/news/nation/pm-rabuka-rejects-narube-call-against-military-leaders)). The stakes reach beyond Fiji: Rabuka has pulled the country closer to Australia and away from the Chinese ties his predecessor built.

**How power works:** A parliamentary republic. The prime minister leads the majority in the 55-seat parliament, elected by open-list proportional representation in a single national constituency; the president, chosen by parliament, is largely ceremonial. The military has historically acted as a political arbiter.

**Who holds power:** Prime Minister Sitiveni Rabuka (People's Alliance), leading a three-party coalition since 2022.

### New Zealand

New Zealand was once the place Australians and Britons imagined escaping to. In 2025 a record number of New Zealanders left for Australia and elsewhere instead, a fact Labour's leader seized on as a verdict on the country's economy ([AAP](https://aapnews.aap.com.au/news/luxon-sets-november-7-as-new-zealand-poll-day)). The country's 2026 was one long election campaign shaped by that mood.

Prime Minister Christopher Luxon, a former Air New Zealand chief executive, announced in January that the election would be held on 7 November, unusually late, which his opponent called a sign of desperation ([1News](https://www.1news.co.nz/2026/01/21/game-on-as-pm-luxon-reveals-2026-election-date/)). It is a rematch of 2023 with the roles reversed: Luxon defends a conservative coalition with the libertarian ACT and Winston Peters's populist New Zealand First, while Labour's Chris Hipkins, who briefly succeeded Jacinda Ardern, tries to return. The backdrop is grim for an incumbent. The economy contracted in three of six quarters, unemployment hit a nine-year high of 5.3 percent, and Luxon has the worst net popularity of any first-term prime minister in recent memory ([AP via ClickOrlando](https://www.clickorlando.com/news/world/2026/01/21/new-zealand-election-scheduled-for-nov-7-with-the-costs-of-living-and-housing-the-top-issue/)). Yet New Zealand rarely throws out a government after one term, and polls show the two blocs close, with the Greens and Te Pāti Māori Labour's likely partners. The vote is four weeks away.

**How power works:** A parliamentary democracy and Commonwealth realm with no written constitution. The prime minister leads the government with a majority in the single-chamber House of Representatives, about 120 seats elected by mixed-member proportional representation, so coalitions are the norm. Elections are held every three years.

**Who holds power:** Prime Minister Christopher Luxon (National), with ACT and New Zealand First.

### Papua New Guinea

Between 1988 and 1998, the island of Bougainville fought a war against Papua New Guinea that began over a giant copper mine, Panguna, whose profits flowed elsewhere while its waste poisoned local rivers. About 20,000 people died, in a population of perhaps 200,000. The peace deal promised a referendum, and in 2019 Bougainvilleans voted 97.7 percent for independence ([bne IntelliNews](https://www.intellinews.com/bougainville-s-independence-battle-reaches-boiling-point-461053/)). The catch is that the vote was non-binding: PNG's national parliament has the final say. 2026 is when that collision came close.

In July, Bougainville's own parliament passed the Kuri Resolution reaffirming independence, with 1 September 2027 as the target date. In August, President Ishmael Toroama told Prime Minister James Marape at a joint meeting that the result could not be "diluted by parliamentary manoeuvres." The two sides did agree that Bougainville would keep 100 percent of revenue raised on the island, from fisheries to mining, by the end of 2026, a step toward the self-sufficiency Marape insists must come first. Marape has worried aloud that independence could set a "dangerous precedent" in a country of more than 800 languages, while vowing PNG's army will never again set foot on Bougainville ([1News](https://www.1news.co.nz/2025/03/28/bougainville-impasse-png-pm-warns-of-dangerous-precedent/)). Around this sits big-power competition: PNG signed the Pukpuk mutual defence treaty with Australia in October 2025, and an Australian-funded PNG rugby league team helped edge out a Chinese security deal ([East Asia Forum](https://eastasiaforum.org/2026/01/21/papua-new-guineas-democracy-at-50/)). A new country could be born in the Pacific next year, or a new crisis.

**How power works:** A parliamentary democracy and Commonwealth realm. The prime minister is elected by the 118-seat National Parliament, chosen by limited preferential voting, and coalitions shift frequently. Bougainville is an autonomous region with its own president and parliament.

**Who holds power:** Prime Minister James Marape (Pangu Pati), in office since 2019.

## Middle East

### Bahrain

Bahrain is a small island ruled by a Sunni royal family over a population that is majority Shia, the same branch of Islam as Iran's. In 2011, when Shia-led protesters filled Manama's Pearl Roundabout calling for reform, Saudi troops crossed the causeway to help crush them. Bahrain also hosts the US Navy's Fifth Fleet. So when the US and Israel went to war with Iran in 2026, the old fault line cracked open.

Bahrain was among the Gulf states hit hardest by Iranian drones and missiles aimed at US forces. Some Shia Bahrainis mourned Ayatollah Khamenei, killed in the opening strikes, or posted sympathy for Iran; a few cheered hits on US bases. The state responded with mass arrests. By late March, rights groups counted more than 200 detained, mostly Shia, many for social media posts or mourning, including some pro-government Shia figures ([AFP via Al-Monitor](https://www.al-monitor.com/originals/2026/03/bahrain-cracks-down-shia-dissent-iran-war-tests-kingdom)). On 9 May, the interior ministry announced the arrest of 41 people it said belonged to a group tied to Iran's Revolutionary Guard, and overnight raids picked up dozens of leading Shia clerics ([AP via ADN](https://www.adn.com/nation-world/2026/05/09/iran-ceasefire-appears-to-hold-and-bahrain-detains-dozens-over-suspected-revolutionary-guard-links/), [The New Arab](https://www.newarab.com/news/bahrain-arrests-dozens-shia-clerics-crackdown-intensifies)). Dozens have been stripped of citizenship. Analysts warn the crackdown could reignite the sectarianism Iran has exploited before, at the moment Bahrain most needs its people united.

**How power works:** A constitutional monarchy in name, with power concentrated in the Al Khalifa family. The king appoints the prime minister, currently Crown Prince Salman, and the 40-member upper house; the elected 40-seat lower house has limited power. The main Shia opposition party, Al-Wefaq, has been banned since 2016.

**Who holds power:** King Hamad bin Isa Al Khalifa, with Crown Prince and Prime Minister Salman bin Hamad.
